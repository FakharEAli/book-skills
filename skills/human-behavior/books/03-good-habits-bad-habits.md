# 03 · Good Habits, Bad Habits: The Science of Making Positive Changes That Stick
**Author(s)**: Wendy Wood | **Year**: 2019 | **Rank in category**: 03/10
**Why it's on the list**: automatic behavior, context cues, friction; why intentions fail

## Core Idea
Roughly 43% of daily actions are habitual: performed automatically in response to stable context cues, with little conscious intent. People suffer an introspection illusion, crediting their goals and willpower for behaviors that are actually run by context. Lasting change therefore comes not from stronger intentions but from engineering context: stable cues, low friction, repetition, and rewards that arrive immediately. The "high self-control" people in Wood's research are mostly people who arranged their environments so that the right behavior was the easy one.

## Frameworks Introduced
**The Habit Formula: Context + Repetition + Reward** — habits form when a behavior is repeated in a consistent context and followed by a reward that is immediate (delayed rewards don't train the habit system) and ideally a little uncertain. **How**: fix the cue (same time/place/trigger), make the first repetitions easy, attach a visible, quick payoff, repeat until the behavior runs without deliberation (Lally's data: median ~66 days, range from 18 to 254 depending on complexity). **Why it works**: it moves the behavior from the goal-directed system to the habit system, which keeps running when motivation lapses. **Failure mode**: rewards that arrive weekly or monthly; cues that move around.

**Friction** — the forces that make a behavior harder or easier (distance, time, effort, number of steps). Wood frames behavior change as Lewin did: reduce restraining forces rather than increase driving forces. **How**: count the steps between cue and completed behavior; cut them; move the desired option physically or digitally closer and the undesired one further. **Why it works**: friction operates on everyone regardless of motivation. **Failure mode**: adding motivation (training, posters, reminders) to a high-friction process.

**The Introspection Illusion** — people believe they act on reasons; much of the time they act on habit and then report reasons. **How**: when a client says "we'll definitely use it", treat it as data about intention, not prediction of behavior; ask instead "where in your day would this sit, and what would it replace?" **Failure mode**: building to stated preferences.

**Habit Discontinuity** — existing habits are disrupted when their context changes (moving house, new job, new premises, new team member); these windows are when new behaviors get adopted. **How**: time rollouts to a disruption (new location, new hire, new season, new software), or create a small one (new device at the front desk). **Failure mode**: inserting a new workflow into an untouched routine and expecting the old cue to stop firing.

**Insensitivity to Outcome** — once formed, habits persist even when the reward is gone. **How**: expect the old process to continue even after your automation makes it pointless; you must remove or repurpose its cue, not just make it unnecessary. **Why it matters**: this is why "but the new way is obviously better" doesn't win.

## Key Principles
- Design for the habit system, not the goal system, because goals fade and habits don't.
- Make the desired behavior the lowest-friction option in the room, because friction beats motivation every time.
- Reward immediately: a visible confirmation, a count, a message that says what just got handled.
- Keep the cue stable: same trigger, same place, same device.
- Replace, don't remove: give the old cue a new response.
- Use disruption windows: new hire, new site, new quarter.
- Expect ~2 months of repetition before a workflow is "theirs"; plan check-ins across that window.
- Distrust stated intentions, including your own; look at what the context makes easy.

## Anti-patterns
- Training sessions as the main adoption tool (they address the conscious system, which isn't the one running the behavior).
- Reminders that arrive when the person can't act.
- Letting the old tool stay visible and reachable while asking people to use the new one.
- Judging adoption at week one; habits aren't formed, so lapses are expected, not failures.

## Worked Example (applied to Fakhar's business)
**Scenario**: Onboarding a 6-person accounting firm onto an AI document-intake workflow (clients email documents; AI classifies, files, and drafts the acknowledgement). Old habit: staff drag attachments into client folders by hand and type a reply.

Habit audit:
- *Cue*: new client email arrives in a shared inbox. Keep it: the automation should trigger from exactly that cue, not require anyone to open a new app.
- *Friction*: old way = 5 steps; new way must be ≤1 (approve draft in the same inbox). If your version needs a dashboard login, it loses.
- *Reward*: immediate — a reply-draft appears with "3 documents filed to Smith & Co. / 2025 Q3" visible in the thread.
- *Replace the old response*: remove the manual folder shortcut from the desktop; the cue (email) now leads to "check draft, hit send".
- *Discontinuity*: launch on the Monday they start tax-season temp staff; new people have no old habit to protect.
- *Repetition*: plan touchpoints at day 3, day 14, day 45; expect lapses in week 2 and treat them as friction signals, not resistance.

**Onboarding promise to the owner**: "Nobody learns a new tool. The email they already open now contains a finished draft. Their only new behavior is pressing send."

## Caveats
The 43% figure comes from diary studies in Wood's lab (student samples); treat it as a magnitude, not a constant. Lally's 66-day median is one study with a small sample. Wood is skeptical of willpower-based models; Fogg (07) agrees but adds the prompt as a distinct variable, and Thaler & Sunstein (04) handle one-off choices, which Wood's framework does not cover. Wood emphasizes repetition; Fogg emphasizes emotion (celebration) as the accelerator, and the two can be combined.
