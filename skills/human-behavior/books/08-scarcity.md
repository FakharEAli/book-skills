# 08 · Scarcity: Why Having Too Little Means So Much
**Author(s)**: Sendhil Mullainathan, Eldar Shafir | **Year**: 2013 | **Rank in category**: 08/10
**Why it's on the list**: tunneling, bandwidth tax, slack

## Core Idea
Scarcity of anything (money, time, calories, company) captures the mind. It produces a **focus dividend** (intense, efficient attention on the scarce resource) and a far larger cost: **tunneling**, where everything outside the immediate shortfall becomes invisible, and a **bandwidth tax**, where cognitive capacity and executive control are consumed by the scarcity itself. The poor and the busy are not less capable people; they are people operating with less mind available. Scarcity then feeds itself through borrowing, juggling, and the absence of **slack**, creating a scarcity trap.

## Frameworks Introduced
**Tunneling** — scarcity narrows attention to the urgent need; items outside the tunnel are not weighed and rejected, they are not seen. **How**: before contacting a time-poor owner, ask what is inside their tunnel this week (payroll, a staff shortage, a bad review, a big job) and present your offer as a fix for *that*, or wait. Things that sit outside the tunnel (long-term efficiency, "transformation") get "tunneling-tax" neglect regardless of merit. **Failure mode**: mistaking silence for rejection and following up with more information rather than more relevance.

**Bandwidth Tax** — scarcity reduces fluid intelligence and executive control. The authors' field evidence: sugarcane farmers scored markedly worse on cognitive tests before harvest (poor) than after (paid), and lab participants primed with a large financial problem showed a similar drop. **How**: design every touchpoint for a person with less working memory than usual: one ask per message, one decision per step, no reading required to act. **Failure mode**: a 12-page proposal to someone who is bandwidth-taxed.

**Slack** — the buffer that absorbs shocks; abundance has slack, scarcity has none, so every small shock becomes a crisis. **How**: sell slack explicitly ("this gives you back six hours a week that aren't allocated to anything") and build slack into your own delivery (never promise a date with zero buffer to a client in a scarcity trap). **Why it works**: slack is what lets the owner *consider* anything new, including your renewal.

**The Scarcity Trap: Juggling and Borrowing** — scarcity leads to borrowing against the future (taking on debt, delaying maintenance, skipping the planning meeting), which creates tomorrow's scarcity. **How**: recognize when an owner's "we'll look at this next quarter" is borrowing; help them see the loop without moralizing. **Failure mode**: judging the owner's "short-termism" as a character flaw (see 02).

**Bringing things into the tunnel / defaults over vigilance** — reminders that arrive at the moment of action, and one-off decisions that remove the need for ongoing vigilance (automatic enrolment, direct debit), work for scarce people; anything that requires sustained attention does not. **How**: make the client's commitment a single decision that then runs itself; never make them re-decide weekly.

## Key Principles
- Treat no-reply as tunneling, not rejection: re-enter with relevance to this week's fire, not with a bump email.
- One ask per message because bandwidth is taxed; a message with two asks gets zero.
- Sell slack, not efficiency: "hours back" that aren't pre-allocated are the owner's scarcest asset.
- Convert vigilance into one-off decisions: approval once, then default-on.
- Time the ask for abundance moments: just after a big invoice is paid, just after the busy season, Tuesday mid-morning rather than Monday or Friday.
- Deliver inside the tunnel: the first automation should remove the specific thing they complained about on the call, not the biggest ROI item.
- Build slack into your own delivery and pricing so you aren't tunneling too.
- Expect bandwidth-taxed clients to make avoidable errors (missed approvals, forgotten logins); design for the error (04), don't blame the person.

## Anti-patterns
- Long proposals, multi-step onboarding, weekly review meetings for owners who are already juggling.
- "Just circling back" follow-ups that add nothing to the tunnel.
- Punishing lapses (late payment fees, strict deadlines) in ways that deepen the scarcity trap and lose the client.
- Abundance-era planning: scoping a project assuming the owner will have time to review it properly.

## Worked Example (applied to Fakhar's business)
**Scenario**: A logistics-company owner took your discovery call, was keen, received the proposal, and has not replied in nine days. Standard move: a "did you get a chance to look?" email. Scarcity move:

1. *Find the tunnel*: from the call notes, two drivers quit and she is covering routes herself. Her tunnel is "get through this week's deliveries".
2. *Enter the tunnel*: one message, one ask, zero reading: "You mentioned you're covering routes yourself. If I set up the automated customer ETA texts this week, that's the 40 'where's my delivery' calls a day gone. Yes/no and I'll start today; the full proposal can wait."
3. *Convert to one-off decision*: she replies "yes"; you don't ask for anything else. You collect what you need from her ops manager; she approves one template.
4. *Deliver slack visibly*: Friday report: "212 ETA texts sent, 0 calls escalated to you."
5. *Return to the proposal from abundance*: the following month, after the ETA system has freed up hours, you present the rest. Now it is inside a wider tunnel.

**Message template for tunneling prospects**: "[Their fire, in their words]. [One thing you can do about it this week]. Reply yes and I start; nothing else needed from you."

## Caveats
The sugarcane-farmer and lab studies have been the subject of replication and reanalysis disputes; the direction (scarcity impairs cognition) has mixed independent support and effect sizes vary. The book is strongest as an interpretive lens for behavior under time and money pressure, weakest as a precise model of IQ-point losses. Wood (03) would note that bandwidth-taxed people default to habit even harder, which is why your automation must sit inside existing habits. Thaler & Sunstein (04) supply the sludge audit that scarcity makes urgent. Sapolsky (01) provides the stress-physiology substrate.
