# 07 · Tiny Habits: The Small Changes That Change Everything
**Author(s)**: BJ Fogg | **Year**: 2019 | **Rank in category**: 07/10
**Why it's on the list**: Fogg Behavior Model (B=MAP), anchors, celebration, behavior design

## Core Idea
A behavior happens when three things converge at the same moment: Motivation, Ability, and a Prompt (B = MAP). Motivation is unreliable and fluctuates in waves, so Behavior Design works mainly on Ability (make it tiny) and Prompts (attach to something that already happens), then uses emotion, not repetition alone, to wire the habit: "emotions create habits". Fogg's recipe is to start absurdly small, anchor the new behavior to an existing routine, and celebrate immediately so the brain tags the behavior as rewarding.

## Frameworks Introduced
**Fogg Behavior Model (B = MAP)** — Behavior = Motivation × Ability × Prompt, converging at one moment; the "action line" curves such that high ability tolerates low motivation and vice versa, but with no prompt nothing happens. **How**: when a behavior isn't happening, troubleshoot in strict order: (1) Is there a prompt? (2) Is the person able to do it right then? (3) Are they motivated? **Why it works**: prompts and ability are cheap to fix and are the usual culprits; motivation is expensive and fickle. **Failure mode**: starting with motivation (incentives, pep talks) because it's the most visible variable.

**Ability Chain (five ability factors)** — time, money, physical effort, mental effort, routine (does it fit what they already do?). The weakest link determines ability. **How**: ask Fogg's Discovery Question, "What is making this behavior hard to do?", for each link; then the Breakthrough Question, "How can I make this behavior easier to do?" **Failure mode**: fixing time when the real block is mental effort or routine fit.

**Tiny Habits Recipe (Anchor → Tiny Behavior → Celebration, "ABC")** — "After I [Anchor: existing routine], I will [Tiny Behavior: the smallest version], then I [Celebrate]." The anchor must be reliable, at the right frequency, and in the right location; the behavior must be doable in under 30 seconds at first; the celebration must be immediate and produce a feeling Fogg calls **Shine**. **Why it works**: the anchor is a prompt that already fires; tininess guarantees ability; celebration supplies the emotion that encodes habit. **Failure mode**: skipping celebration because it feels silly; picking an anchor that doesn't occur in the same place as the behavior.

**Behavior Design process: Clarify Aspiration → Explore Behavior Options (Swarm of Behaviors) → Match with Specific Behaviors (Focus Mapping) → Start Tiny → Find a Good Prompt → Celebrate → Troubleshoot, Iterate, Expand** — the full method. **How**: Focus Mapping plots candidate behaviors by impact (vertical) and feasibility (horizontal); **Golden Behaviors** are high-impact, ones the person actually wants to do, and ones they can do. **Failure mode**: choosing the highest-impact behavior that nobody wants to do.

**Three prompt types: Person, Context, Action** — person prompts (remembering) fail; context prompts (reminders, notes) are unreliable; action prompts (one existing behavior triggers the next) are the most dependable. **How**: never rely on the client remembering; attach the new step to an existing action.

**Untangling bad habits** — reverse B=MAP: remove the prompt, make the behavior harder, reduce motivation; in that order. **How**: to kill the old manual process, first remove its prompt (the sticky note, the open spreadsheet tab), then add friction.

## Key Principles
- Troubleshoot Prompt → Ability → Motivation, because that is cheapest-first and most-likely-first.
- Make the client's first new behavior under 30 seconds; scale it after it's automatic.
- Use action prompts: "After you [existing step], you [new step]"; never "remember to".
- Celebrate on their behalf: the system's confirmation message *is* the celebration; make it feel like a win, not a log entry.
- Pick Golden Behaviors: impactful, wanted, feasible. A wanted medium-impact behavior beats an unwanted high-impact one.
- Design for the motivation trough, not the motivation wave: assume the owner's launch-day enthusiasm will be gone in a week.
- Ask the Discovery Question before every redesign: "What is making this hard?"
- Expand only after the tiny version is automatic; premature scope expansion kills the habit.

## Anti-patterns
- Launching the full workflow on day one and expecting adoption ("go big" is the failure mode Fogg's whole method is built against).
- Reminder emails as the prompt strategy (context prompts; they decay).
- Treating low usage as a motivation problem and responding with training or incentives.
- Judging people for lapses; Fogg's design stance is "behavior change is a design problem, not a character problem".

## Worked Example (applied to Fakhar's business)
**Scenario**: Designing week-one adoption for an AI quoting assistant for a 12-person plumbing company. The aspiration: "quotes go out the same day". Swarm of behaviors: engineers record job details, office approves drafts, owner reviews pricing, customers accept online, and so on.

Focus Mapping: the Golden Behavior is "office manager approves the AI-drafted quote" (high impact, she wants it: it removes typing; feasible: one tap).

Recipe: "After I finish the morning job allocation (anchor, daily, same desk), I will open the quotes queue and approve one draft (tiny behavior), then I'll see the 'Sent, 3 min after allocation' banner (celebration)."

Ability Chain check: time (30 seconds, OK), money (none), physical (same screen), mental (draft is pre-written; she edits, not creates), routine (allocation already happens at 8:15 daily). Weakest link was routine fit; fixed by anchoring to allocation rather than to "when a job finishes".

Prompt: action prompt (allocation done → queue opens automatically in the same tab). No reminders.

Untangle the old habit: remove the "Quotes" spreadsheet shortcut from her desktop (prompt removal); archive the Word template (add friction).

Expansion schedule: week 1 one quote a day; week 2 all quotes; week 4 engineers submit voice notes directly.

**Onboarding script**: "For the first week you do one thing: approve one draft after allocation. If that feels easy by Friday, we add the rest."

## Caveats
Fogg's evidence is largely from his own Tiny Habits coaching programs and Stanford lab, not large independent trials; the model is a design heuristic, not a validated predictive equation. Wood (03) would argue repetition and context stability do more than celebration, and that "emotions create habits" overstates one mechanism; the two combine well in practice. Thaler & Sunstein (04) cover one-off choices Fogg's model doesn't address. The Tiny Habits brand and some vocabulary (Shine, Swarm of Bs) are proprietary; use them as names, not as evidence.
