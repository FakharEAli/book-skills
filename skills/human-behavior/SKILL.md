## Core Frameworks & Mental Models

### 1. Behavior is situational, not dispositional
**The Three Pillars: Situationism, Construal, Tension Systems** (Ross & Nisbett) — behavior is driven by the situation, by how the person *interprets* it, and by opposing forces held in equilibrium. → When a client stalls, ask "what in their situation changed?" before "what kind of person are they". Prefer changing the situation over persuading the person because situational effects are large and trait effects are weak.

**Fundamental Attribution Error** (Ross & Nisbett) — we over-explain others' behavior by character and under-explain by circumstance. → When you think "this owner is flaky", you are probably wrong about the cause and about the fix. Look for the channel that is missing.

**Channel Factors** (Lewin via Ross & Nisbett) — small concrete pathways (a map, a time slot, a pre-filled form) that convert intention into action. → Intention without a channel decays to zero. Every "yes" you get needs a dated, specific next step booked *in that moment*.

**Nested Timescales of Behavior** (Sapolsky) — any act has causes one second before (brain state), minutes before (cues, stress), hours before (hormones), years before (development), and generations before (culture). → Don't read a single rude email as personality; read it as a stressed frontal cortex. Delay the ask, lower the cognitive load, re-send when their load is lower (Tuesday morning, not Friday 5pm).

**Us/Them switching** (Sapolsky) — the brain sorts people into in-group/out-group in milliseconds, and the categories are fluid. → Lead with shared identity (same industry, same frustrations) before the pitch; "Them" gets scrutinized, "Us" gets trusted.

### 2. Habits, friction, and why intentions fail
**Context → Cue → Response → Reward** (Wood) — habits are automatic responses to stable context cues, built by repetition and immediate reward; intentions barely touch them. → Your automation competes with an existing habit (e.g., texting the booking to the receptionist). Replace the cue, don't lecture the person.

**Friction (driving vs restraining forces)** (Wood, after Lewin) — reducing restraining forces beats adding motivation. → Adoption design = remove steps. If the client has to open a new tool, you've lost; put the output where they already look (WhatsApp, their inbox, their POS).

**Fogg Behavior Model: B = MAP** (Fogg) — Behavior happens when Motivation, Ability, and a Prompt converge at the same moment. Troubleshoot in order: Prompt → Ability → Motivation. → When adoption fails, first check: did a prompt fire? Then: was it easy? Only last: did they care?

**Tiny Habits Recipe: Anchor → Tiny Behavior → Celebration** (Fogg) — "After I [existing routine], I will [tiny new behavior]", then celebrate immediately. → Anchor the client's new workflow to an existing one ("After you close the till, you tap Approve on the day's drafts").

**Ability Chain** (Fogg) — time, money, physical effort, mental effort, routine fit; the weakest link limits the behavior. → Audit your onboarding against all five. Mental effort and routine fit are the usual killers.

**Habit Discontinuity** (Wood) — habits break when context changes (new premises, new hire, new system). → Time your rollout to a natural disruption; selling into a stable routine is uphill.

### 3. Scarcity, bandwidth, and choice architecture
**Tunneling & the Bandwidth Tax** (Mullainathan & Shafir) — scarcity (of time, money) focuses attention on the urgent and taxes cognitive capacity; things outside the tunnel are not rejected, they are invisible. → A busy owner not replying is not a "no". Bring your proposal *into* the tunnel: frame it as the fix for the fire they're fighting this week, and make the decision a one-off, not a vigilance task.

**Slack** (Mullainathan & Shafir) — margin that absorbs shocks; its absence creates the scarcity trap. → Sell slack ("this gives you back 6 hours a week") more than efficiency. Build slack into your own delivery timelines.

**Choice Architecture & Defaults** (Thaler & Sunstein) — people take the path of least resistance; the default wins. → Pre-configure everything. Offer one recommended package with opt-outs, not a blank menu.

**NUDGES checklist** (Thaler & Sunstein) — iNcentives, Understand mappings, Defaults, Give feedback, Expect error, Structure complex choices. → Run every onboarding flow and pricing page through it.

**Sludge** (Thaler & Sunstein) — friction that makes the good choice harder (forms, approvals, logins). → Audit your own sales and onboarding process for sludge before you blame the client.

### 4. Moral intuition, social brain, and hidden motives
**Intuitions first, strategic reasoning second** (Haidt) — the elephant (intuition) decides, the rider (reason) rationalizes. → Talk to the elephant first: trust, story, demo; then supply the rider with reasons it can repeat to a partner.

**Moral Foundations** (Haidt) — Care, Fairness, Loyalty, Authority, Sanctity, Liberty. → Owners often resist automation on Loyalty ("my staff") or Liberty ("I don't want to be dependent"). Address the active foundation, not the one you'd use.

**Social pain is real pain; mentalizing is the default mode** (Lieberman) — the brain's resting state is thinking about other people; social rewards and threats are processed like physical ones. → Status threat to the owner or their team kills deals silently. Frame adoption so the owner looks good to the people they care about.

**Prestige vs Dominance; Prestige-biased learning** (Henrich) — humans copy the prestigious, the successful, and the self-similar; credibility-enhancing displays (CREDs) matter more than claims. → Case studies from *their* kind of business beat any feature list. Do the costly thing visibly (use your own automations publicly).

**Hidden Motives & Signaling** (Simler & Hanson) — stated reasons are the press secretary's version; the real motive is often status, loyalty, or looking modern. → When a client buys "AI", ask what they want to *be seen as*. Sell the signal honestly, deliver the substance.
