# 10 · Let's Get Real or Let's Not Play
**Author(s)**: Mahan Khalsa, Randy Illig | **Year**: 1999 (revised 2008) | **Rank in category**: 10/10
**Why it's on the list**: Helping Clients Succeed; move off the solution, evidence/impact/context/constraints; yellow lights; no guessing

## Core Idea
The FranklinCovey "Helping Clients Succeed" approach holds that the consultant and the client want the same thing, a solution that exactly meets the client's needs, and that most sales dysfunction comes from both sides pretending otherwise. The seller's job is to make the conversation real: get off the solution the client arrived with, find out whether a problem worth solving exists, surface every concern early, and refuse to guess. If the deal is not real, the seller says so and walks, which paradoxically builds the trust that wins the next one. Intent counts more than technique.

## Frameworks Introduced
**ORDER** — the sequence of what must be understood before proposing: **O**pportunity (is there a real problem or result worth pursuing?), **R**esources (time, people, money available), **D**ecision process (who decides, how, when, on what criteria), **E**xact solution (only now, and co-designed), **R**esults (how success will be measured and reviewed). **How**: do not propose until O, R and D are clear; most stalled proposals skipped D. **Failure mode**: writing the Exact solution after the first call.

**Move off the solution** — clients arrive with a solution in mind ("we need a chatbot"). Acknowledge it, then get underneath: "What would that solve for you?" Repeat until you reach the business result. **Why**: a solution without an owned problem has no value anchor and gets price-shopped.

**Issues → Evidence → Impact → Context → Constraints** — for each issue the client raises, ask for **Evidence** (how do you know it's a problem; what do you see), **Impact** (what it costs in measurable terms; who else is affected), **Context** (what has been tried; what is going on around it), **Constraints** (what would stop a fix from working). **How**: do this for every issue before asking which matters most, then let the client prioritise.

**Yellow lights** — any signal of hesitation, vagueness or incongruity is a yellow light: slow down and name it rather than speed through. "I noticed a pause when I mentioned the pilot fee. What's behind that?" **Why**: unspoken concerns become the "let me think about it" at the end; surfaced early they are solvable.

**No guessing** — never fill in a client's meaning, budget, timeline or decision process from your own assumptions. If you do not know, ask. If asking feels awkward, say that it feels awkward and ask anyway.

**Qualify out loudly** — if the opportunity is not real, or you cannot win or deliver, say so plainly and early. "Let's get real or let's not play."

## Key Principles
- Get off the solution first because the client's proposed solution is a hypothesis, not a requirement.
- Ask for evidence before impact because impact built on an unverified issue collapses under scrutiny later.
- Collect every issue before diving on one; the first issue mentioned is rarely the most important.
- Name yellow lights immediately because unnamed hesitation grows into a stall.
- Never guess at budget or decision process; ask directly, with permission if needed ("Would it be all right if I asked about budget?").
- Clarify the decision process before the proposal; the proposal is for the decider, not the contact.
- Say what you are not good for; it earns the right to be believed about what you are good for.
- Do not propose into ambiguity; an ambiguous yes is a slow no.

## Anti-patterns
- Writing the proposal the client asked for without establishing the result it must produce.
- Hearing "budget is flexible" and not asking what flexible means in numbers.
- Pushing past a hesitation because the meeting is going well.
- Guessing the decision-maker is the person on the call.
- Presenting a solution before Opportunity, Resources and Decision are real.

## Worked Example (applied to Fakhar's business)
Inbound lead: operations director at a 60-person accounting firm, "We want an AI assistant for client onboarding. Can you send a proposal?"
- **Move off the solution**: "Happy to. So I build the right thing, what would an onboarding assistant solve for you?" → "Onboarding takes three weeks." → "What does three weeks cost you?" → "We lose clients between signing and first deliverable, and partners hate the chasing."
- **Evidence**: "How many clients dropped in the last quarter during onboarding?" **Impact**: "At your average first-year fee, what's that?" **Context**: "What have you tried?" (a checklist in the PM tool; nobody follows it). **Constraints**: "What would stop a fix from sticking?" (partners won't use another tool).
- **Yellow light**: a pause at "partners". Name it: "You hesitated on partners. Is there a concern about whether they'd go along?" → the real issue: a partner killed the last tool.
- **Resources**: "Is there a budget set aside, or would this come out of something else?" Ask for a range.
- **Decision**: "Who besides you needs to say yes, and what will they want to see?"
- **Exact solution**: co-design in a second call with the sceptical partner present. **Results**: agree the metric (onboarding under 10 days; drop-offs under 1%) before pricing.
- If any of O/R/D stays vague after a direct question: "I'd rather not send a proposal that guesses; it would waste your time. Can we get those two things clear first?"

## Caveats
- Practitioner methodology from FranklinCovey; no independent research base.
- Its collaborative stance can be misused as passivity; the Challenger Sale (06) and JOLT (02) remind you that recommending and limiting are also forms of helping the client succeed.
- Overlaps with SPIN (01) and Gap Selling (04) on evidence and impact; the distinctive contributions are "move off the solution", yellow lights and the ORDER sequence.
