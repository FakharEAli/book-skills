# 09 · The Science of Selling
**Author(s)**: David Hoffeld | **Year**: 2016 | **Rank in category**: 09/10
**Why it's on the list**: six whys, buying commitments, science-backed questioning, emotional states

## Core Idea
Hoffeld's thesis is that selling should align with how the brain actually forms buying decisions, drawing on social psychology, behavioural economics and neuroscience rather than sales folklore. A purchase is not one decision but a sequence of smaller commitments, each answering a specific "why" in the buyer's mind. The seller's job is to identify which why is unanswered, create the mental and emotional conditions for the buyer to answer it, and secure a small commitment before moving on.

## Frameworks Introduced
**The Six Whys** — the questions a buyer must answer, roughly in order, before they buy: (1) **Why change?** (2) **Why now?** (3) **Why your industry solution?** (the category, e.g. automation vs hiring) (4) **Why you and your company?** (5) **Why your product or service?** (6) **Why spend the money?** **How**: map every objection to the why it belongs to and answer that why, not the surface complaint; sequence your discovery and presentation to resolve them in order. **Why it works**: a buyer who has not answered "why change" cannot meaningfully evaluate "why you"; skipping whys produces stalls that look like price objections. **Failure mode**: pitching "why us" to a buyer still on "why change".

**Buying commitments / commitment objectives** — define, for every interaction, the specific small commitment you want (agree the problem is worth solving; agree to share data; agree to a stakeholder meeting). **How**: ask for it explicitly; a commitment answered aloud is consistent with later behaviour (commitment and consistency). **Why**: large decisions are made as chains of small public commitments.

**Science-backed questioning** — ask questions in a deliberate progression, from facts to meaning to emotion, and use the buyer's own answers as the basis for the next question; questions guide attention, and what the buyer attends to feels important. Peripheral-route cues (likeability, confidence, social proof) matter alongside central-route arguments.

**Emotional states** — buyers in a positive emotional state are more receptive and more likely to commit; negative states (stress, irritation, distraction) suppress buying. **How**: read the state at the start; if it is negative, address it before selling (acknowledge, reschedule, or shift it with a relevant positive); link your proposal to the emotions the buyer associates with the future state.

**Inoculation against objections** — raise and answer the likely objection yourself before the buyer does, so the buyer builds resistance to the competitor's version of it.

## Key Principles
- Diagnose which of the six whys is unanswered before responding to any objection; "it's too expensive" is usually an unanswered why change or why now.
- Ask for a specific commitment at the end of every call; momentum is a series of small yeses said aloud.
- Order your questions from facts to meaning to feeling because the brain needs the earlier layer to answer the later one.
- Check the buyer's emotional state first; selling into a bad mood lowers the odds and burns the relationship.
- Use social proof that matches the buyer (same size, same industry) because similarity, not volume, drives its effect.
- Inoculate: say the competitor's best argument before they do and answer it.
- Give reasons: a stated "because" raises compliance even when the reason is modest.

## Anti-patterns
- Treating all objections as price objections.
- Ending calls with "I'll send over some info" (no commitment).
- Presenting features and ROI to a stressed buyer who has just come out of a bad meeting.
- Using big-brand logos as social proof with a 15-person company.

## Worked Example (applied to Fakhar's business)
Objection mapping on a proposal call with a logistics-brokerage owner who says "It's a lot of money for software".
- **Diagnose the why**: ask, "Is the question whether the problem is worth fixing at all, whether now is the time, or whether this is the right way to fix it?" He answers that his dispatchers "manage fine". That is **why change**, not why spend.
- **Return to why change** with his numbers: "You said two dispatchers spend their first two hours each morning re-keying load confirmations. At your loaded cost that's roughly X a year, before errors."
- **Why now**: "You're adding a third dispatcher in Q1; is the plan to have them re-key too?"
- **Why industry solution**: automation vs hiring, in his terms.
- **Why us**: one matched proof: "a 20-truck broker in the same lane set-up".
- **Inoculate**: "The obvious objection is 'what if the carrier's email format changes?' Here's how the parser handles it and who fixes it when it doesn't."
- **Commitment objective** for this call: agreement that the re-keying is worth eliminating and a date with the ops lead. Ask for both aloud.

## Caveats
- Hoffeld cites a broad set of psychology studies; some (priming effects, certain embodied-cognition findings) have weak replication records. Treat the six whys and commitment sequencing as useful structure, not proven mechanism.
- Overlaps with Cialdini (sibling human-psychology skill) and with SPIN (01); its distinctive contribution is the ordered why-diagnosis of objections.
- Written for product sellers with a defined offering; adapt "why your product" to "why this scope".
