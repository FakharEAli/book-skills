# 08 · To Sell Is Human
**Author(s)**: Daniel H. Pink | **Year**: 2012 | **Rank in category**: 08/10
**Why it's on the list**: ABCs: Attunement, Buoyancy, Clarity; pitch types; servant selling

## Core Idea
Most people now spend much of their working time in "non-sales selling": persuading, influencing and moving others without a transaction. Because buyers now have as much information as sellers (caveat venditor replaces caveat emptor), the old ABC of "Always Be Closing" fails. Pink replaces it with Attunement, Buoyancy and Clarity: the ability to see from the other person's perspective, to stay afloat through rejection, and to help people see their situation in a new light, particularly by finding the right problem rather than solving the stated one.

## Frameworks Introduced
**The new ABCs** — **Attunement**: bring yourself into harmony with the other person. Reduce your sense of power (lower-power people take perspective better), use your head as much as your heart (perspective-taking beats empathy for outcomes), and mimic strategically (match posture, pace and words after a short delay). Ambiverts outsell both extroverts and introverts. **Buoyancy**: before, during and after. Before: interrogative self-talk ("Can I do this? How?") beats declarative ("I can do this") because it surfaces strategy. During: positivity ratio, more positive than negative emotion, but keep some negativity for realism. After: an optimistic explanatory style, treating rejection as temporary, specific and external. **Clarity**: move from problem *solving* to problem *finding*; curate information rather than supply it; use contrast to clarify; give people an off-ramp (a clear next action).

**Framing for clarity (five frames)** — the **less frame** (fewer options), the **experience frame** (sell the experience, not the thing), the **label frame** (assign a positive identity: "you're the kind of operator who…"), the **blemished frame** (a small negative after a list of positives increases credibility), the **potential frame** (potential is more compelling than achievement when you can make the future vivid).

**Six successors to the elevator pitch** — the **one-word pitch**; the **question pitch** (when your argument is strong, a question makes them generate the reasons); the **rhyming pitch**; the **subject-line pitch** (utility or curiosity, plus specificity); the **Twitter pitch** (short enough to repeat); the **Pixar pitch** ("Once upon a time… Every day… One day… Because of that… Because of that… Until finally…").

**Improvise** — hear offers (listen without planning your reply), say "yes, and" to build, make your partner look good.

**Serve** — make it personal (be a person to them; let them be a person to you) and make it purposeful (tie the ask to a purpose beyond the deal). The test: if the person you are selling to agrees, will their life improve, and will the world be a little better?

## Key Principles
- Enter calls with interrogative self-talk because it generates the plan; affirmations do not.
- Lower your status cues in discovery because feeling powerful reduces perspective-taking.
- Find the problem the buyer has not articulated; solving the stated one commoditises you.
- Curate: three relevant facts beat a dossier.
- Use the question pitch when your case is strong and the one-word pitch for positioning.
- Add a small blemish to your offer after the strengths; perfect offers read as sales copy.
- End every interaction with an off-ramp: one specific, easy next action.
- Explain rejections as temporary and specific; it keeps you prospecting.

## Anti-patterns
- "Always Be Closing" energy with informed buyers; it triggers reactance.
- Empathy without perspective-taking: feeling for the owner but not understanding their constraints.
- Dumping information to look thorough; it reduces clarity.
- Pitches that describe you rather than make the listener think.

## Worked Example (applied to Fakhar's business)
Cold outreach to a dental-clinic owner, built on Clarity and the pitch types.
- **Subject-line pitch** (utility + specificity): "27 missed calls last Tuesday, probably"
- **Question pitch** (case is strong): "If a third of your new-patient calls go to voicemail during lunch and after 5pm, what does that cost in a month?"
- **Problem finding**: "Most practices think the fix is more front-desk hours. In the ones we've looked at, the problem is that calls cluster when the desk is busiest, so hours don't help; instant text-back and booking does."
- **Blemished frame**: "It won't handle complex treatment questions; those still go to your team. It does catch the 'can I book a cleaning' calls that make up most of what you miss."
- **Off-ramp**: "Reply with your main line number and I'll send you a count of how many after-hours calls hit it last week. No call needed."
- **Buoyancy**: track replies per 50 sends; a no-reply is specific and temporary, not a verdict.

## Caveats
- Several studies Pink leans on have replication problems: the 3:1 positivity ratio (Fredrickson and Losada) has been mathematically discredited; power-posing has failed replication; the ambivert sales study is a single sample.
- The frameworks (ABC, pitches, frames) are useful synthesis rather than Pink's own research; treat them as checklists, not laws.
- "Serve" is the book's ethical core and aligns with Khalsa (10) and Maister (03); it is also the most frequently skipped part.
