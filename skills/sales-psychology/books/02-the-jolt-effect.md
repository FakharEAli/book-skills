# 02 · The JOLT Effect
**Author(s)**: Matthew Dixon, Ted McKenna | **Year**: 2022 | **Rank in category**: 02/10
**Why it's on the list**: customer indecision (not status quo) loses deals; Judge the indecision, Offer a recommendation, Limit the exploration, Take risk off the table; pings and pongs

## Core Idea
Analysing roughly 2.5 million recorded sales conversations, Dixon and McKenna found that a large share of qualified pipeline (they report 40–60%) ends in "no decision", and that most of those losses are not to the status quo but to *customer indecision*: the buyer agrees change is needed, then cannot commit. Indecision is driven by omission bias, the fear of messing up (FOMU), not by insufficient fear of missing out. Average sellers respond by piling on more FOMO and more information, which deepens the paralysis. High performers do the opposite: they diagnose the indecision and actively take decision risk off the customer.

## Frameworks Introduced
**Beating the status quo vs. beating indecision** — two separate jobs. Status quo is defeated by making the case for change (why leave the present). Indecision is defeated by making the choice feel safe (why this, why now, what if it fails). **Failure mode**: re-running the business case for change when the customer is already convinced of change and stuck on the *choice*.

**Three sources of indecision** — (1) **valuation problems**: too many options, cannot tell which is best; (2) **lack of information**: wants to research more before committing; (3) **outcome uncertainty**: doubts the promised result will materialise. **How**: identify which one dominates, because each needs a different JOLT lever.

**JOLT** — **J**udge the indecision: assess level and type early, using "pings" (low-stakes seller statements or questions that invite a telling reaction) and reading the "pongs" (how the customer responds, e.g. deflection, vagueness, deferral, requests for ever more detail). Qualify on ability to decide, not just on need and budget. **O**ffer a recommendation: once needs are understood, stop asking "what would you like?" and give a clear personal recommendation ("Given what you've told me, I'd start with X and leave Y for phase two"). **L**imit the exploration: act as the trusted expert who owns the information flow; anticipate objections, answer the questions that matter, and tell the customer when further research will not change the answer. **T**ake risk off the table: offer downside protection (pilots, opt-outs, phased scope, guarantees, defined exit criteria) and *set realistic expectations*, even lowering the promised outcome, because overpromising raises outcome uncertainty.

## Key Principles
- When a buyer says "let me think about it", first decide whether it is a status quo problem or an indecision problem; the cures are opposite.
- Dialling up FOMO on an indecisive buyer backfires because their dominant fear is already making a mistake.
- Giving more information to a "lack of information" buyer prolongs the loop; curate and close the loop instead.
- Recommend, do not menu. A buyer facing three equally argued packages is a valuation problem you created.
- Make the first commitment small and reversible when outcome uncertainty is high; expansion follows success.
- Underpromise deliberately: a credible 30% is worth more than an exciting 60% the buyer does not believe.
- Qualify for decisiveness: a prospect with huge need and no history of deciding is a worse bet than a smaller, decisive one.
- Time kills: every added week of "exploration" lowers win probability; set and defend a decision date.

## Anti-patterns
- Answering "I need to think about it" with more case studies, a longer demo or a bigger ROI deck.
- Presenting three tiers with equal enthusiasm and letting the buyer pick.
- Promising transformational results to win the deal; it increases outcome uncertainty and later churn.
- Treating an unlimited pilot as risk reduction: an open-ended pilot is itself a form of exploration without end.

## Worked Example (applied to Fakhar's business)
Proposal stage; the owner has agreed the manual quoting process costs them, has seen a demo, and now says "Let me think about it and talk to my team."
1. **Judge** (ping): "Totally fair. Can I ask what you'll be weighing up: whether to do this at all, which of the options, or whether it'll actually work in your shop?" The pong tells you the type. "Whether it'll actually work" = outcome uncertainty.
2. **Offer**: "Then here's my recommendation. Don't buy the full build. Start with the quote-drafting agent on one estimator for 30 days. That's where 80% of the saving sits."
3. **Limit**: "You mentioned looking at two other vendors. Happy to walk through how we compare on the three things that matter for you: integration with ServiceTitan, accuracy on edge-case jobs, and who fixes it when it breaks. Beyond those, more research won't change the picture."
4. **Take risk off**: "Pilot fee is fixed. If the agent isn't drafting 70% of quotes with no edits by day 30, you pay nothing further and keep the workflow docs. I'd rather you expect 70% and see 85% than the reverse."
5. Close for an Advance: "Shall we book the 30-day review with your lead estimator now?"

## Caveats
- The conversation corpus is proprietary (Tethr) and the analysis is not peer-reviewed; the 40–60% no-decision figure is a practitioner estimate.
- Written for B2B software reps; the "limit the exploration" move can read as pushy with owner-operators who buy on relationship. Pair it with The Trusted Advisor (03) so you earn the standing first.
- Gap Selling (04) and SPIN (01) assume the problem is insufficient perceived value; JOLT shows value can be fully established and the deal still dies. Diagnose before choosing a book.
