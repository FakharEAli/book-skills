## Core Frameworks & Mental Models

### 1. Find the job and the segment before you design anything
- **Jobs to Be Done** (Christensen, Hall, Dillon, Duncan) — a customer "hires" a product to make progress in a specific circumstance; every job has functional, social and emotional dimensions → Use when writing the first line of any offer: name the progress, not the automation. Prefer "circumstance + progress" over "persona + feature" because the same owner hires different solutions in different circumstances.
- **Four Forces of Progress** (Moesta & Engle) — Push of the situation + Pull of the new solution must exceed Anxiety of the new solution + Habit of the present → Use when a prospect stalls: find which force is weak, then change the offer (guarantee, pilot, done-for-you onboarding) to lower anxiety and break habit rather than piling on more pull.
- **Buying Timeline** (Moesta & Engle) — First Thought → Passive Looking → Active Looking → Deciding → Onboarding → Ongoing Use → Use when choosing what to say in outreach vs on a call: passive lookers need their struggling moment mirrored back; active lookers need trade-offs and proof.
- **Value Proposition Canvas** (Osterwalder, Pigneur, Bernarda, Smith) — Customer Profile (jobs, pains, gains, ranked) ↔ Value Map (products & services, pain relievers, gain creators); "fit" when the map hits the top-ranked items → Use when auditing a draft offer: strike every deliverable that does not relieve a ranked pain or create a ranked gain.
- **Starving Crowd** (Hormozi) — pick a market with massive pain, purchasing power, easy targeting and growth → Use when choosing the industry for the first offer; prefer a niche where the owner already pays for the problem (payroll, VA, agency fees, lost leads) over one that is merely curious about AI.

### 2. Position so the alternatives look weak
- **Positioning Components** (Dunford) — Competitive Alternatives → Unique Attributes → Value (and proof) → Best-fit Customers → Market Category → optional Relevant Trend, worked in that order → Use when positioning against "hire a VA / use Zapier / do nothing": the alternatives are what the buyer would actually do, not other AI agencies.
- **Market frame styles** (Dunford) — Head-to-head, Big Fish Small Pond, Create a New Game → Prefer Big Fish Small Pond ("AI intake automation for dental groups") over Create a New Game because a sub-segment already has a budget line and a vocabulary.
- **Four Actions Framework / ERRC Grid** (Kim & Mauborgne) — Eliminate, Reduce, Raise, Create against the industry's factors of competition; draw it on a **Strategy Canvas** → Use when your offer reads like every other "AI agency": eliminate hourly billing and open-ended scoping, raise speed-to-live and client ownership, create an outcome guarantee.
- **Value Innovation** (Kim & Mauborgne) — differentiation AND lower cost at once; a good strategy has focus, divergence and a compelling tagline → Use as the final test: if your value curve tracks the competitors', you are competing on price.

### 3. Build the offer itself
- **Value Equation** (Hormozi) — Value = (Dream Outcome × Perceived Likelihood of Achievement) ÷ (Time Delay × Effort & Sacrifice) → Use to raise value without discounting: shrink the denominator first (done-for-you setup, live in 14 days, zero owner time) because that is where AI services beat a VA.
- **Grand Slam Offer build** (Hormozi) — list every problem on the way to the dream outcome → turn each into a solution → pick delivery vehicles → trim to high-value/low-cost → stack → Use once you have job and segment and need the deliverable list; never start from "what can n8n do".
- **MAGIC naming** (Hormozi) — Make a Magnetic reason why, Announce the avatar, Give them a goal, Indicate a time interval, Complete with a container word → Use for the offer headline.
- **Enhancers** (Hormozi) — Scarcity, Urgency, Bonuses, Guarantees (unconditional, conditional, anti-guarantee, implied) → Prefer adding a bonus over dropping the price; prefer a conditional guarantee tied to a measurable outcome you control.
- **Big Four Questions + Touchstone** (Joyner) — the buyer silently asks "What are you trying to sell me? How much? Why should I believe you? What's in it for me?" and gives you about three seconds; the Touchstone is one sentence that answers them → Use for the first line of a landing page, cold message or proposal cover.
- **Diagnose Before You Prescribe / Do Not Solve Problems Before You Are Paid** (Enns) — sell a paid diagnostic before any build → Use when a prospect wants a "free scoping call" that is really free consulting.

### 4. Price and test before you build
- **Willingness-to-Pay talk early** (Ramanujam & Tacke) — ask price questions during discovery, not after the build: direct WTP questions, purchase-probability questions, acceptable/expensive/prohibitive ranges → Use before writing a single workflow: "If this took intake fully off your desk, what monthly figure feels acceptable? Expensive? Prohibitive?"
- **Four monetization failures** (Ramanujam & Tacke) — Feature Shock, Minivation, Hidden Gem, Undead → Use as the post-mortem taxonomy when an offer flops; most agency offers are Minivations (under-ambitious, underpriced).
- **Leaders, Fillers, Killers** (Ramanujam & Tacke) — bundle around a leader; drop killers that lower WTP for the whole bundle → Use when deciding whether a chatbot belongs in an ops-automation offer.
- **Good-Better-Best / anchoring** (Ramanujam & Tacke; Enns on multiple options) — present three options; most buyers pick the middle → Use on every proposal; put the option you want sold in the middle.
- **Evidence strength ladder** (Bland & Osterwalder) — what people say < what they click < what they pre-pay; test Desirability → Feasibility → Viability → Use to pick the next test: interviews first, then a landing page or mock sale, then a presale or letter of intent before building.
- **Test Card / Learning Card** (Bland & Osterwalder; Osterwalder et al.) — hypothesis, test, metric, success criterion; then observation, learning, decision → Use to keep offer tests honest.
