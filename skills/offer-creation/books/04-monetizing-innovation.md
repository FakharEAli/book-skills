# 04 · Monetizing Innovation: How Smart Companies Design the Product Around the Price
**Author(s)**: Madhavan Ramanujam, Georg Tacke | **Year**: 2016 | **Rank in category**: 04/10
**Why it's on the list**: willingness-to-pay talks before building; the 9 rules; feature shock, minivation, hidden gem, undead; price-setting strategies

## Core Idea
Most new offerings fail commercially because price is decided last, after the product is built and the costs are sunk. Ramanujam and Tacke (Simon-Kucher partners) argue you must find out what customers value and will pay for before you build, then design the product, packaging and price around that evidence. Pricing is not a number at the end; it is the design constraint at the start.

## Frameworks Introduced
**The Nine Rules** — **How**: (1) Have the willingness-to-pay (WTP) talk early. (2) Do not default to one-size-fits-all; segment by WTP and needs. (3) Treat product configuration and bundling as science (leaders, fillers, killers). (4) Choose the right monetization model (subscription, usage, freemium, alternate metric, dynamic, auction, etc.). (5) Pick a pricing strategy deliberately: maximization, penetration or skimming. (6) Build an outside-in business case from WTP, not an inside-out one from cost. (7) Communicate value, not price (benefit-led messaging). (8) Use behavioral pricing tactics (anchors, good-better-best, decoys, price endings). (9) Maintain price integrity; avoid knee-jerk discounting. **Why / failure**: each rule prevents a specific failure; most agencies break rules 1, 2, 6 and 9 at once.

**Four types of monetization failure** — **How** (diagnose which you have): Feature Shock: too many features, confused buyer, price cannot cover it. Minivation: the right idea priced and scoped too timidly; it sells but leaves most value on the table. Hidden Gem: real value the company does not recognise or sell because it sits outside the core. Undead: a product nobody wanted, kept alive by sunk cost. **Why / failure**: naming the failure type tells you the fix (cut, raise price, spin out, kill).

**WTP conversation methods** — **How**, in order of effort: Direct questions ("What would you consider an acceptable price? An expensive price? A prohibitively expensive price?"). Purchase-probability questions ("On a 1–5 scale, how likely would you be to buy at X?"). Most-least (which feature matters most, least). Build-your-own (let them assemble a package and see what they include). Van Westendorp price sensitivity meter (too cheap / bargain / expensive / too expensive across respondents). Conjoint analysis for larger samples. **Why / failure**: asking "would you buy this?" yields politeness; asking for the price at which it becomes expensive yields a range you can work with.

**Leaders, Fillers, Killers (bundling)** — **How**: Leader = feature customers would buy on its own and that drives WTP. Filler = nice-to-have that adds a little value and cost. Killer = feature a meaningful share of buyers actively does not want; including it lowers WTP for the whole bundle. Build the bundle around leaders, add fillers to differentiate tiers, remove killers or make them optional add-ons. **Why / failure**: bundles usually fail by including a killer to look complete.

**Pricing strategies** — **How**: Maximization: price at the level that maximises profit now (default for a capacity-constrained service). Penetration: deliberately low to win share fast, with a plan to raise. Skimming: start high for early adopters, step down. Choose explicitly and write down the trigger for changing it. **Why / failure**: agencies drift into penetration pricing without deciding to and never raise.

## Key Principles
- Ask price questions in the first two conversations, because the answer changes what you should build, not just what you charge.
- Segment buyers by what they value and will pay, because a single package forces the high-WTP buyer to pay less and the low-WTP buyer to walk.
- Build the business case from the customer's economics outward (value of a lead × leads recovered × margin), not from your hours inward.
- Offer good-better-best; buyers anchor on the middle option, and the top option makes the middle look reasonable.
- Price on the value metric the buyer already counts (locations, leads, hours removed), not on nodes or workflows.
- Never discount the price; if you must concede, remove scope, because a discount teaches the buyer the real price is lower.
- Communicate value first and price second in every document, because a price seen before its value is always too high.
- Decide maximization vs penetration explicitly and write the date you will revisit it.

## Anti-patterns
- Building the system first and "figuring out pricing later".
- One package, one price, for every client size.
- Cost-plus or hourly pricing for outcome-bearing work.
- Including a killer feature (a chatbot the owner's staff dislike) to make the bundle look complete.
- Reacting to the first pushback with a discount.
- Asking "would you pay for this?" instead of asking for price ranges.

## Worked Example (applied to Fakhar's business)
Discovery call with a 2-location dental group owner, after the struggling moment is clear:
"Before I scope anything, I want to make sure I build something that is worth it to you. If this answered every enquiry within five minutes and booked the consult, what monthly figure would feel acceptable? … What would start to feel expensive? … What would be prohibitive, where you would not even consider it?" Answers: acceptable 1,500; expensive 3,000; prohibitive 5,000 (per month).
Outside-in case: 40 missed or late enquiries a month, 25% would have booked, average first-year patient value 1,200 → roughly 12,000 a month of recoverable revenue. The middle option can sit at 2,500/month with a 6,000 build fee and still look conservative.
Bundle: Leader = after-hours lead response and booking. Fillers = weekly report, reminder sequence. Killer (from most-least) = a patient-facing chatbot on the website; make it an optional add-on.
Good-better-best: Essential (one location, email and web forms only, 1,500/mo), Complete (all locations, all channels, reminders, 2,500/mo), Front-Desk Replacement (plus insurance pre-check and recall campaigns, 4,000/mo). Present Complete as "what most groups your size choose".
Price integrity: if they push, offer Essential, not a cheaper Complete.

## Caveats
- The authors' evidence is Simon-Kucher client work, mostly large product companies; the methods are sound but the quantitative tools (conjoint, Van Westendorp) need samples a niche agency rarely has. Use the direct and purchase-probability questions and treat answers as directional.
- Stated WTP is still what people say; Bland & Osterwalder (09) rank it below a presale or deposit.
- Enns (06) agrees on value pricing but would run the money conversation differently: as a value conversation about the client's outcome, before any price is named.
- Behavioral tactics (decoys, price endings) can read as manipulative in a relationship sale; use good-better-best, skip the tricks.
