# 09 · Testing Business Ideas: A Field Guide for Rapid Experimentation
**Author(s)**: David J. Bland, Alexander Osterwalder | **Year**: 2019 | **Rank in category**: 09/10
**Why it's on the list**: experiment library; desirability/feasibility/viability; evidence strength

## Core Idea
Most business ideas fail not because the team cannot build them but because nobody wanted, could deliver, or could profit from them. Instead of building and hoping, you break the idea into hypotheses about desirability (do customers want it), feasibility (can we deliver it) and viability (can we make money), then run the cheapest experiment that produces the strongest evidence for the riskiest hypothesis. The book is a library of 44 experiments with their cost, time, evidence strength and the hypotheses they test.

## Frameworks Introduced
**Hypothesis types: Desirability, Feasibility, Viability** — **How**: write each hypothesis as "We believe that [customer] will [behaviour] because [reason]"; tag it D, F or V; rank by importance and by how little evidence you have; test the riskiest important one first. For a services offer, desirability (will this segment buy this outcome) and viability (at this price, with this delivery cost) usually dominate; feasibility is rarely the risk for an experienced builder. **Why / failure**: teams test feasibility first because it is comfortable; the offer then works perfectly for nobody.

**Evidence strength** — **How**: rank evidence from weak to strong: opinions and what people say (interviews, surveys) < what people say they would do (stated intent) < what people do in a controlled setting (clicks, sign-ups) < what people do with real money or commitment (pre-orders, deposits, letters of intent, paid pilots). Require stronger evidence as the cost of being wrong rises. Never make the build decision on interview evidence alone. **Why / failure**: the failure is treating enthusiastic interviews as validation.

**Discovery vs validation experiments** — **How**: Discovery experiments (cheap, fast, weak-to-medium evidence) find and sharpen the idea: customer interviews, discovery surveys, search-trend analysis, a day in the life, expert interviews, paper prototypes, storyboards, explainer videos, brochures, landing pages, online ads, link tracking. Validation experiments (costlier, stronger evidence) confirm the decision: mock sale, presale, letter of intent, concierge, Wizard of Oz, single-feature MVP, pop-up, crowdfunding, split test, validation survey. Sequence discovery → validation; do not skip to validation before the proposition is sharp. **Why / failure**: running a presale on an unsharpened offer produces a "no" you cannot interpret.

**Test Card / Learning Card and the experiment sequence** — **How**: Test Card before: hypothesis, experiment, metric, success criterion (a number, decided in advance). Learning Card after: observation, insight, decision (persevere, pivot, kill, or test more). Chain experiments so each result picks the next. **Why / failure**: a pre-committed criterion is the only defence against post-hoc rationalisation.

**Concierge and Wizard of Oz** — **How**: Concierge: deliver the outcome manually and visibly to a few paying customers to learn what they value before automating. Wizard of Oz: deliver the outcome with a human behind the curtain while the customer experiences it as the product. Both are strong evidence (real money, real use) at low build cost. **Why / failure**: for an automation agency these are the natural validation step: run the "AI intake" for one client half-manually for two weeks before building the full system.

## Key Principles
- Test the riskiest assumption first, which for a new offer is almost always "this segment will pay this price for this outcome", not "we can build it".
- Decide the success number before the test, because afterwards every number looks like success.
- Prefer behaviour to opinion; a 15-minute deposit beats a 60-minute interview.
- Run cheap discovery before expensive validation; sharpen the proposition on interviews and a landing page before asking for money.
- Use a concierge or Wizard of Oz pilot to earn revenue and evidence before you build the automation.
- Run several small experiments in sequence rather than one big launch, because each result tells you what to test next.
- Count real commitments (deposits, signed LOIs, calendar time with the owner), not clicks, when the decision is "build or not".
- Kill or pivot when the criterion is missed; "we learned a lot" is not a decision.

## Anti-patterns
- Building the full system as the "test".
- Interview enthusiasm treated as validation.
- No pre-committed success criterion.
- Validation experiments (presale) on an unsharpened proposition.
- Testing with friendly contacts who will not be buyers.
- Running one experiment and generalising from it.

## Worked Example (applied to Fakhar's business)
Idea: "The 14-Day Five-Minute-Reply System for multi-location clinics" at 6,000 build + 2,500/month.
Hypotheses: D1: owners of 2–5 location clinics consider slow enquiry response a top-three problem. D2: they prefer a done-for-you system to hiring more front-desk staff. V1: at least 3 in 10 qualified owners will pay a deposit at the proposed price. F1: median reply under five minutes is achievable across their channels (low risk; test last).
Sequence:
1. Discovery: 8 timeline interviews (07) with clinic owners who recently hired staff or tried a chatbot. Test Card: "We are right if 6 of 8 describe a lost-enquiry struggling moment unprompted." Weak evidence, cheap, sharpens the language.
2. Discovery: landing page with the Touchstone (05), good-better-best pricing (04) and a "book a 20-minute fit call" button; drive 100 targeted visitors via LinkedIn posts and a small ad. Criterion: 8+ fit calls booked.
3. Validation: mock sale on the fit calls; present the offer and ask for a refundable deposit to reserve a build slot. Criterion: 3 of 10 pay.
4. Validation: concierge pilot for the first 2 depositors: run intake semi-manually with a simple agent and human review for 14 days at the full monthly fee. Criterion: both renew into the build; median reply under five minutes achieved.
5. Only then build the productised system.
Learning Card after step 3, if 1 of 10 pays: observation: owners wanted a one-location trial; insight: anxiety about multi-site rollout; decision: add a one-location Essential tier and re-run.

## Caveats
- The library is written for product teams in companies with budgets; many experiments (crowdfunding, pop-up store, 3D print) do not apply to services. The applicable core is interviews, landing page, ads, mock sale, presale, letter of intent, concierge, Wizard of Oz.
- Evidence-strength rankings are the authors' judgement, not measured reliability.
- Small samples: a niche agency may only reach 10–20 qualified owners; set criteria as counts, not percentages, and accept the uncertainty.
- Enns (06) would warn that free "pilots" and unpaid landing-page experiments can position you as a vendor; keep pilots paid.
