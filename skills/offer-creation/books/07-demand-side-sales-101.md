# 07 · Demand-Side Sales 101: Stop Selling and Help Your Customers Make Progress
**Author(s)**: Bob Moesta with Greg Engle | **Year**: 2020 | **Rank in category**: 07/10
**Why it's on the list**: Jobs to Be Done; four forces (push, pull, anxiety, habit); the timeline of a buying decision; struggling moments

## Core Idea
Supply-side selling starts from the product and pushes features; demand-side selling starts from the customer's struggling moment and helps them make progress. People do not buy products; they hire them to move from a current situation to a better one, and the buying decision follows a predictable timeline shaped by four forces. If you understand where the buyer is on that timeline and which forces are in play, you stop persuading and start helping them decide.

## Frameworks Introduced
**The Four Forces of Progress** — **How**: for every prospect map: Push of the situation (what is wrong now, the struggling moment); Pull of the new solution (the attraction of the better life); Anxiety of the new solution (what could go wrong if I switch); Habit of the present (the comfort of the current way, however bad). Progress happens when Push + Pull > Anxiety + Habit. Diagnose the weakest or strongest force and act on it: amplify push by making the cost of now visible; sharpen pull by showing the specific better day; reduce anxiety with pilots, guarantees, references, done-for-you onboarding; break habit by removing switching effort. **Why / failure**: sellers default to adding pull (more benefits); most stalled deals are anxiety or habit problems that more pull cannot fix.

**The timeline of a buying decision (six stages)** — **How**: First Thought (something plants the idea; a trigger event) → Passive Looking (noticing options, no urgency) → Active Looking (comparing, building criteria, trade-offs) → Deciding (narrowing, negotiating with self and others) → Onboarding / First Use (does it work as hoped) → Ongoing Use (habit forms or the product is fired). Identify the stage from how the prospect talks: passive lookers describe the struggle; active lookers ask comparison questions; deciders ask about risk and terms. Sell to the stage: give passive lookers language for their struggle, active lookers trade-offs and criteria, deciders risk reduction. **Why / failure**: pitching features to a passive looker or struggling-moment stories to a decider wastes the conversation.

**Struggling moments** — **How**: in interviews and discovery, hunt for the moment the buyer first said "I can't keep doing this": the event, the context, who was there, what they tried. Use the buyer's own words in copy and outreach. **Why / failure**: demand is created by the struggle, not the solution; copy that describes the solution without the struggle attracts no one.

**Timeline interview (demand-side interview)** — **How**: with recent buyers (or recent "firers" of a VA or tool), walk the timeline backwards: "When did you first think about this? What was happening? What did you look at? What almost stopped you? What finally made you decide?" Record pushes, pulls, anxieties, habits and trade-offs in their language. **Why / failure**: reconstructs the causal story of the purchase; failure is asking hypothetical questions ("would you buy…") instead of reconstructing what actually happened.

**Big Hire and Little Hire** — **How**: the Big Hire is the purchase; the Little Hire is each moment of use. Design onboarding so the first little hires succeed quickly; otherwise the product is fired despite the sale. **Why / failure**: an AI system that goes live but is not used is fired silently; the retainer churns.

## Key Principles
- Find the struggling moment before you describe anything, because the buyer's energy to change comes from the push, not your pull.
- Diagnose which stage of the timeline the prospect is in and give them what that stage needs, because the same content helps in one stage and hurts in another.
- When a deal stalls, name the anxiety out loud ("what would need to be true for you to feel safe doing this?") because unnamed anxieties do not shrink.
- Reduce anxiety with structure, not reassurance: a pilot, a reversible step, a guarantee, a reference they can call.
- Respect habit: the current way works well enough or they would have changed; show the switching path, not the destination.
- Help the buyer see their trade-offs; buyers do not want everything, they want the thing that solves the struggle without new problems.
- Design the first week of use for quick little hires, because retention is decided in onboarding.
- Use the buyer's language from interviews in every message; your category words create no demand.

## Anti-patterns
- Leading with features or capabilities to a buyer who has not articulated a struggle.
- Treating objections as pull problems and answering with more benefits.
- Hypothetical interview questions ("would you use…").
- Ignoring habit, then wondering why the "obviously better" option lost to doing nothing.
- Celebrating the big hire and neglecting onboarding.

## Worked Example (applied to Fakhar's business)
Prospect: owner of a 15-person recruitment agency, interested for six weeks, not moving.
Timeline read: she described the struggle vividly (a candidate lost because a reply sat for two days), compared you with "a part-time coordinator" and "the ATS's built-in automation" (Active Looking → Deciding).
Forces: Push strong (lost placements). Pull moderate (she likes the idea but has not pictured the better day). Anxiety high: "What if it says something wrong to a candidate? What if it breaks and nobody notices? What if my consultants ignore it?" Habit: consultants already text candidates from their phones.
Response: do not add pull. Lower anxiety: offer a 14-day shadow mode where the agent drafts replies that a consultant approves before sending, with a daily digest of what it would have done; guarantee that nothing is sent unapproved until she switches it; monitoring alert to her phone. Break habit: the agent writes into the same channels consultants already use, so nothing changes for them on day one. Amplify push gently: "How many of the last ten placements you lost had a reply delay in them?"
Onboarding (little hires): day-one win is the daily digest arriving at 8am; week-one win is the first candidate booked without a consultant touching it.
Copy for the passive-looker LinkedIn audience: lead with the struggling moment ("The candidate had accepted elsewhere by the time your reply went out"), not the system.

## Caveats
- Moesta's evidence is case-based (consumer purchases, education, housing); the frameworks are practitioner heuristics, not controlled research.
- The timeline is linear in the book; B2B buying with several stakeholders loops and splits, so map each stakeholder separately.
- Overlaps with 08 (Christensen) on JTBD language; Moesta's contribution here is the forces and the timeline, Christensen's is job discovery and the job spec.
- "Stop selling" can be over-read; Enns (06) still wants you to lead the conversation and ask for money early.
