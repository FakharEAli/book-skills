# 02 · Value Proposition Design: How to Create Products and Services Customers Want
**Author(s)**: Alexander Osterwalder, Yves Pigneur, Gregory Bernarda, Alan Smith | **Year**: 2014 | **Rank in category**: 02/10
**Why it's on the list**: Value Proposition Canvas: customer jobs, pains, gains ↔ products, pain relievers, gain creators; fit

## Core Idea
A value proposition is the bundle of products and services that creates value for a specific customer segment, and it can be designed deliberately rather than guessed. The Value Proposition Canvas puts the customer's jobs, pains and gains on one side and your products, pain relievers and gain creators on the other; fit exists only when the right side addresses the items the customer ranks highest. Fit is a hypothesis until evidence from the market confirms it.

## Frameworks Introduced
**Customer Profile (right side of the canvas)** — **How**: (1) Customer Jobs: list functional jobs (tasks to complete), social jobs (how they want to be seen), emotional jobs (how they want to feel), plus supporting jobs (buyer, co-creator, transferrer of value). (2) Pains: undesired outcomes, obstacles, risks; note severity (extreme ↔ moderate). (3) Gains: required, expected, desired, unexpected; note relevance (essential ↔ nice-to-have). (4) Rank each column: jobs by importance, pains by severity, gains by relevance. **Why / failure**: ranking is the whole point; an unranked profile makes every deliverable look justified.

**Value Map (left side)** — **How**: (1) Products & Services: everything you offer, tagged essential or nice-to-have. (2) Pain Relievers: how each product eliminates or reduces a specific pain. (3) Gain Creators: how each product produces a specific gain. Draw a line from every reliever/creator to a ranked pain/gain; anything unlinked is a candidate to cut. **Why / failure**: forces one-to-one accountability between deliverable and customer item; failure is writing relievers that are features in disguise ("uses AI").

**Fit (three stages)** — **How**: Problem-Solution Fit = on paper, the map addresses the top-ranked jobs, pains, gains. Product-Market Fit = evidence that customers respond (sign-ups, pre-orders, paid pilots). Business Model Fit = the proposition can be delivered profitably and repeatably. **Why / failure**: most offers are declared "fit" at stage one; stage one is just a sharper hypothesis.

**Value proposition ad-lib** — **How**: "Our [products and services] help(s) [customer segment] who want to [job to be done] by [reducing / avoiding] [pain] and [increasing / enabling] [gain], unlike [competing value proposition]." Write five versions, each leading with a different top-ranked job. **Why / failure**: fast way to prototype alternative propositions; failure is treating the sentence as final copy rather than a prototype.

**Test Card and Learning Card** — **How**: Test Card: "We believe that… To verify, we will… and measure… We are right if…" Learning Card: "We believed… We observed… From that we learned… Therefore we will…" **Why / failure**: makes the success criterion explicit before the test runs; without it every result feels like confirmation. Expanded in 09.

## Key Principles
- Rank jobs, pains and gains before designing anything, because the proposition that wins addresses a few extreme pains, not many moderate ones.
- Write pains in the customer's words from interviews, not in your category's words, because "manual data entry" is your term and "I re-type every lead into three systems and still miss some" is theirs.
- Separate functional from social and emotional jobs; the owner's emotional job ("stop feeling like the bottleneck") often justifies the price more than the functional one.
- Tag each product or service essential or nice-to-have, and build the core offer from the essentials only.
- Treat the first canvas as a hypothesis set and move to evidence quickly; a beautiful canvas is still an opinion.
- Prototype several value propositions for the same segment and compare, rather than refining one.
- Include the buyer's jobs around buying (comparing, justifying to a partner, getting sign-off) because unmet buying jobs block otherwise-fit propositions.

## Anti-patterns
- Listing every possible job, pain and gain without ranking.
- Writing pain relievers that restate features ("automated workflows") rather than name the pain removed.
- Designing for a vaguely defined segment ("SMB owners") so the profile is a blend of people with different jobs.
- Mistaking the team's enthusiasm for fit.
- Addressing pains the customer rates as moderate because they are easy to solve.

## Worked Example (applied to Fakhar's business)
Segment: owner-operator of a 10–30 person home-services company (HVAC, plumbing) handling inbound leads personally.
Customer Profile (ranked): Jobs — functional: respond to every lead and book an estimate; social: look responsive and professional compared with competitors; emotional: stop being on-call for the phone. Pains — extreme: leads lost to faster competitors; weekend and evening enquiries unanswered; moderate: double entry into CRM and calendar; mild: reporting is manual. Gains — essential: more booked estimates from the same ad spend; expected: nothing breaks silently; desired: see lead flow on the phone; unexpected: win back old quotes.
Value Map: Products — lead-response agent (essential), calendar booking (essential), CRM sync (essential), weekly lead report (nice-to-have), dashboard (nice-to-have). Pain relievers — "answers every lead on every channel within two minutes, 24/7" (extreme pains 1 and 2); "writes the lead into your CRM and calendar once, correctly" (moderate pain). Gain creators — "books estimates directly into open slots" (essential gain); "alerts you if any step fails" (expected gain); "re-engages quotes older than 30 days" (unexpected gain → becomes a bonus in the 01 build).
Ad-lib: "Our 24/7 lead-response system helps owners of growing HVAC companies who want to book every estimate by eliminating lost after-hours leads and increasing booked estimates per ad dollar, unlike hiring an answering service that cannot book or qualify."
Cut: the dashboard, until an interviewee ranks "see lead flow" above moderate.

## Caveats
- The canvas is a design and communication tool; it contains no method for discovering jobs, which 07 and 08 supply, and no method for pricing, which 04 supplies.
- The book is written for product companies and workshops; for a small agency the full workshop process is heavy, and the ad-lib plus a ranked profile is usually enough.
- Fit stages are described, not measured; the evidence thresholds are left to you (see 09 for evidence strength).
- Dunford (03) would add that the "unlike" clause must name what the buyer would really do instead, not a rival vendor.
