# 10 · Blue Ocean Strategy: How to Create Uncontested Market Space and Make the Competition Irrelevant
**Author(s)**: W. Chan Kim, Renée Mauborgne | **Year**: 2005 (expanded edition 2015) | **Rank in category**: 10/10
**Why it's on the list**: strategy canvas; eliminate-reduce-raise-create grid; value innovation

## Core Idea
Red oceans are existing markets where everyone competes on the same factors and margins shrink; blue oceans are new market space created by redefining what the offering competes on. The route is value innovation: raising buyer value and lowering your cost at the same time by eliminating and reducing factors the industry over-invests in and raising and creating factors it ignores. The tools make the current industry's offer visible so you can diverge from it deliberately.

## Frameworks Introduced
**Strategy Canvas** — **How**: (1) list the factors the industry competes on (x-axis): for AI agencies, perhaps breadth of integrations, custom scoping, hourly rates, tool expertise, model sophistication, dashboards, support hours. (2) Plot the offering level of the incumbents on each (y-axis, low to high) to draw their value curve; also plot the buyer's other alternatives (VA, Zapier DIY). (3) Draw your intended curve. A good curve has focus (a few factors high, not all), divergence (shape differs from the competitors'), and a compelling tagline that follows from it. **Why / failure**: if your curve tracks the competitors' with every factor slightly higher, you are in the red ocean competing on price.

**Four Actions Framework and the ERRC Grid** — **How**: for the factors on the canvas ask: Eliminate: which factors the industry takes for granted should be removed? Reduce: which should be well below the industry standard? Raise: which should be well above? Create: which factors has the industry never offered? Fill the grid with concrete items; eliminate and reduce cut your cost, raise and create lift buyer value. **Why / failure**: the failure is only raising and creating, which produces a premium offering at a premium cost, not value innovation.

**Six Paths Framework (where to look for blue oceans)** — **How**: look across (1) alternative industries (staffing agencies, SaaS, consultants), (2) strategic groups (freelancers vs boutique agencies vs enterprise consultancies), (3) the chain of buyers (owner vs office manager vs accountant), (4) complementary products and services (training, change management, monitoring), (5) functional vs emotional appeal, (6) time and trends. **Why / failure**: systematic alternatives to waiting for inspiration; each path surfaces factors the industry ignores.

**Buyer Utility Map and the sequence of blue ocean strategy** — **How**: six stages of the buyer experience cycle (purchase to disposal) × six utility levers (productivity, simplicity, convenience, risk, fun and image, environmental friendliness); find the blocked cells. Then validate in order: buyer utility → strategic price (the price corridor of the mass) → target cost → adoption hurdles. **Why / failure**: utility before price and price before cost, the reverse of cost-plus thinking (consistent with 04).

**Three tiers of noncustomers** — **How**: first tier: soon-to-be noncustomers at the edge of your market who buy minimally; second tier: refusing noncustomers who considered and rejected the category; third tier: unexplored noncustomers no one has targeted. Find what they share rather than what differentiates existing customers. **Why / failure**: growth for a niche agency often sits in owners who rejected "AI" after a bad chatbot (second tier), not in competing for the few already buying.

## Key Principles
- Draw the industry's value curve before designing yours, because you cannot diverge from what you have not made visible.
- Eliminate and reduce as aggressively as you raise and create, because the cuts are what let you offer more at a price the buyer accepts and still earn margin.
- Aim for focus: three or four factors far above the industry, several far below; a flat "better at everything" curve is a red-ocean curve.
- Write the tagline last and check it falls out of the curve; if you cannot state the curve in a sentence, it lacks focus.
- Look at the chain of buyers: the office manager who lives with the system daily may value different factors than the owner who signs.
- Study noncustomers who rejected the category; their reasons are the factors to eliminate or create.
- Sequence utility → price → cost → adoption; never start with your cost.

## Anti-patterns
- Benchmarking against competitors and outdoing them slightly on every factor.
- Only raising and creating without eliminating and reducing.
- Technology-driven differentiation buyers do not value (a better model, more nodes).
- Pricing from cost.

## Worked Example (applied to Fakhar's business)
Factors AI agencies compete on (canvas x-axis): breadth of use cases offered; custom scoping per client; hourly or day-rate billing; number of integrations; model and tool sophistication talk; dashboards and reporting; discovery workshops; ongoing support hours.
Incumbent curve: high on breadth, custom scoping, integrations, tool talk, dashboards; medium on support; billing by time.
Alternatives: VA: high on flexibility and human judgement, low on 24/7, consistency, scalability. Zapier DIY: high on cheapness and control, low on reliability, reasoning over messy input, monitoring.
ERRC for the clinic lead-response offer:
Eliminate: hourly billing; open-ended scoping; tool and model talk in sales materials; the discovery workshop.
Reduce: breadth (one job: enquiry → booked); integrations to the three channels that carry 90% of enquiries; dashboards to a daily digest.
Raise: speed to live (14 days, fixed); reliability (monitoring with human fallback); owner control (approval mode, voice-and-rules document); ownership (client owns the build).
Create: a reply-time guarantee; a published fixed price; a 14-day shadow mode before go-live; staff onboarding in 30 minutes.
Resulting curve: focused (speed, reliability, control, guarantee high; breadth, scoping, tool talk low), divergent from both agencies and the VA, and the tagline follows: "Every enquiry answered and booked in five minutes, live in 14 days, guaranteed."
Six Paths check: chain of buyers: the practice manager cares most about "nothing breaks silently"; make the alert digest hers. Tier-two noncustomers: owners burned by a website chatbot; shadow mode and human handoff exist for them.
Sequence: utility confirmed in interviews (07) → price between a VA's cost and a full-time receptionist (04) → cost fits because scope was cut → staff resistance handled by the created factors.

## Caveats
- Kim and Mauborgne's cases (Cirque du Soleil, Yellow Tail, Southwest) are successes analysed after the fact; critics note survivorship bias. Use the tools for clarity, not as a promise of uncontested space.
- For a one-offer agency, the canvas and ERRC grid are the useful parts; the organisational and execution chapters matter less.
- Dunford (03) and Kim & Mauborgne agree on divergence but Dunford would warn against "Create a New Game" positioning; the ERRC grid can produce an offer that still lives inside a known category (Big Fish, Small Pond), which is the safer reading.
- Hormozi (01) adds factors (bonuses, guarantees) that are "Create" items; the ERRC grid gives them a disciplined home.
