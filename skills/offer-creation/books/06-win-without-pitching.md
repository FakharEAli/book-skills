# 06 · The Win Without Pitching Manifesto
**Author(s)**: Blair Enns | **Year**: 2010 | **Rank in category**: 06/10
**Why it's on the list**: 12 proclamations: specialize, replace presentations with conversations, diagnose before prescribing, charge for diagnostics, pricing based on value

## Core Idea
Creative and consulting firms give away their thinking in pitches because they have positioned themselves as interchangeable vendors. Enns argues the way out is to become the expert: specialise narrowly, lead the engagement as a practitioner leads a patient, talk about money early, refuse to work for free, and price on the value you create rather than the hours you spend. The manifesto is twelve proclamations about how an expert firm sells.

## Frameworks Introduced
**The Twelve Proclamations** — **How**: (1) We Will Specialize: pick a discipline, a market or both, narrow enough that you can claim expertise. (2) We Will Replace Presentations with Conversations: no speculative decks; two-way diagnostic dialogue. (3) We Will Diagnose Before We Prescribe: no solution before understanding the problem. (4) We Will Rethink What It Means to Sell: selling is helping the client make a decision, not persuading. (5) We Will Do With Words What We Used to Do With Paper: proposals confirm what was agreed in conversation; they do not sell. (6) We Will Be Selective: qualify hard, say no. (7) We Will Build Expertise Rapidly: publish, teach, narrow. (8) We Will Not Solve Problems Before We Are Paid: the diagnostic is a paid engagement. (9) We Will Address Issues of Money Early: budget and value in the first conversations. (10) We Will Refuse to Work at a Loss. (11) We Will Charge More: price on value with options. (12) We Will Hold Our Heads High: the expert's posture, detached from the outcome of any one deal. **Why / failure**: each proclamation removes a vendor behaviour; the failure is adopting (11) without (1), (3) and (8), which just makes you an expensive vendor.

**Paid diagnostic (the first engagement)** — **How**: when a prospect asks for a proposal, propose a short paid engagement instead: a scoped audit of their lead flow, workflows or ops with a written findings-and-recommendation document and a price. Price it low enough to be an easy yes, high enough to signal expertise. The recommendation is the proposal for the build. **Why / failure**: converts free consulting into revenue, filters non-buyers, and lets you prescribe from evidence. Failure: making the diagnostic so cheap or so large that it becomes the build.

**The expert's posture (practitioner, not vendor)** — **How**: ask more than you tell; set the agenda; be willing to say "this is not a fit"; do not chase. Hold the next step: you propose the diagnostic, you set the price, you decide whether to proceed. **Why / failure**: buyers defer to whoever is leading; the firm that pitches has already conceded the lead.

**Value pricing with options** — **How**: anchor on the value of the outcome discussed in the money conversation; offer multiple options at different price points and scopes; never a single number; never hourly. **Why / failure**: a single price is a yes/no; options turn the decision into "which", and the top option sets the anchor. Enns later expanded this into a full pricing method; the manifesto gives the principle.

## Key Principles
- Specialise until you can say who you are for in one breath, because expertise is only perceived relative to a narrow field.
- Replace the "capabilities deck" with a diagnostic conversation, because the client learns more about your expertise from your questions than from your slides.
- Never scope on the first call; propose a paid diagnostic, because free scoping is free consulting and the buyer discounts what was free.
- Raise money in the first or second conversation ("what is this problem costing you; what would solving it be worth; is there a budget range in mind?"), because late money talk wastes both sides' time and positions you as the one who was afraid to ask.
- Qualify hard and decline mismatches politely, because selectivity is itself evidence of expertise.
- Price every engagement with options, because the client needs a choice and you need an anchor.
- Publish what you know (LinkedIn, newsletter) to build expertise rapidly, because the expert is the one the market has already heard from.
- Do not chase; the follow-up is the client's job once you have given them the decision to make.

## Anti-patterns
- Writing speculative proposals or free "audits" to win work.
- Presenting a solution in the first meeting.
- Leaving money to the proposal.
- Pricing hourly or with a single number.
- Taking any client who will pay, which erodes the specialism and the posture.
- Chasing silent prospects with "just checking in".

## Worked Example (applied to Fakhar's business)
Prospect (owner, 25-person accounting practice) emails: "Can you send a proposal for automating our client onboarding?"
Vendor reply: a deck and a quote. Expert reply (Proclamations 2, 3, 8, 9):
"Happy to. Before I can price a build responsibly I need to see how onboarding actually runs: where documents arrive, who touches them, where it stalls. I do that as a one-week Onboarding Diagnostic: I interview you and two staff, map the current flow, measure the hours and delays, and give you a written recommendation with two or three build options and prices. The diagnostic is [fee], credited against the build if you go ahead. Two questions first so I know it is worth your money: roughly how many new clients a month, and what does a slow onboarding cost you, in staff hours or lost clients?"
Diagnostic output doubles as the proposal: findings, three options (Essential / Complete / Fully Managed), each with scope, price and the value math from the conversation. Delivered in a call, not emailed cold (Proclamation 5). If they decline the diagnostic, that is the qualification working.
Posture: no "checking in" emails; one message a week after the call: "Have you decided whether to proceed with the diagnostic? Either answer is fine."

## Caveats
- The manifesto is written for creative agencies pitching for accounts; its tone (proclamations) is motivational and offers little tactical detail, which Enns supplies in later books.
- Paid diagnostics assume you already have some reputation; a brand-new agency may need one or two free pilots to earn the posture, which conflicts with Proclamation 8. Treat the free pilot as a 09-style experiment with explicit success criteria, not as free consulting.
- Hormozi (01) and Joyner (05) sell with aggressive offers and intensifiers; Enns would see much of that as vendor behaviour. For outbound and web copy use 01/05; for the conversation, use Enns.
- "Charge more" without the WTP evidence of 04 is a posture, not a price.
