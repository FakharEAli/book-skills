# 03 · Obviously Awesome: How to Nail Product Positioning so Customers Get It, Buy It, Love It
**Author(s)**: April Dunford | **Year**: 2019 | **Rank in category**: 03/10
**Why it's on the list**: positioning: competitive alternatives, unique attributes, value, best-fit customers, market category; the 10-step process

## Core Idea
Positioning is the context you set so that a buyer immediately understands what you are, why it matters and who it is for. Most products are positioned by default, in the category the founder first imagined, and so are compared with the wrong alternatives and judged on the wrong criteria. Dunford's method works backwards from the customers who already love you to find the market frame that makes your strengths obvious.

## Frameworks Introduced
**The Five (plus one) Components of Positioning** — **How**, worked strictly in this order because each feeds the next: (1) Competitive Alternatives: what the best-fit customer would do if you did not exist (often "do nothing", a spreadsheet, an intern, a cheaper tool). (2) Unique Attributes: capabilities you have that the alternatives lack. (3) Value (and proof): what those attributes enable for the customer, with evidence. (4) Target Market Characteristics: the traits of buyers who care most about that value. (5) Market Category: the frame of reference you place yourself in so the value is assumed. (+1) Relevant Trends: optional context that makes the category feel timely; never a substitute for category. **Why / failure**: the failure is starting with the category ("we're an AI agency") and then trying to justify it; the category should fall out of the value.

**The 10-step positioning process** — **How**: (1) Understand the customers who love your product (who, why, what they compare you to). (2) Form a positioning team across sales, marketing, product, leadership. (3) Align your positioning vocabulary and let go of positioning baggage (old category assumptions). (4) List your true competitive alternatives from the customer's view. (5) Isolate your unique attributes. (6) Map attributes to value themes (cluster into 2–4 themes). (7) Determine who cares a lot (best-fit customer characteristics). (8) Find a market frame of reference that highlights your strengths; choose a style. (9) Layer on a trend, carefully. (10) Capture the positioning in a document so sales, marketing and product can use it. **Why / failure**: skipping steps 1 and 4 produces positioning based on what you wish you were; skipping step 10 means it never reaches the proposal or the website.

**Three styles of market frame** — **How**: Head-to-Head: compete to win an existing category (requires being able to beat the leader on the category's own criteria). Big Fish, Small Pond: take an existing category and dominate a sub-segment the leader underserves ("X for Y"). Create a New Game: define a new category and teach the market its criteria (expensive, slow, needs a real trend behind it). **Why / failure**: small companies default to Create a New Game because it feels bold; it is usually the most expensive choice. Big Fish, Small Pond is the default for a niche services firm.

**Positioning canvas / document** — **How**: one page: competitive alternatives, unique attributes, value themes with proof, best-fit customer characteristics, market category, relevant trend. Hand it to whoever writes the website, proposals and outreach. **Why / failure**: positioning that lives only in the founder's head drifts with every conversation.

## Key Principles
- Start from the customers who love you and ask what they would have done without you; their answer is the competitive alternative your positioning must beat.
- Treat "do nothing" and "hire someone" as real competitors with real strengths (no vendor risk, familiar, flexible) and position against those strengths honestly.
- Only count an attribute as unique if the alternatives genuinely lack it; "we use AI" is not unique against Zapier plus ChatGPT.
- Translate every attribute into value for the buyer and attach proof; attributes without value are a feature list, value without proof is a claim.
- Describe the best-fit customer by characteristics that predict caring ("owner handles leads personally", "multi-location", "already paying a VA"), not by demographics.
- Choose the smallest market frame you can dominate and widen later; being the obvious choice for 500 clinics beats being one option for 50,000 businesses.
- Use a trend only to explain why now; if the trend is doing the work of the category, buyers will not know what you are.

## Anti-patterns
- Positioning statement templates filled in with aspirations rather than evidence from real customers.
- Listing other vendors as competitive alternatives when the buyer's actual alternative is the status quo.
- Claiming a new category to avoid comparison; the buyer then compares you anyway, to whatever they know.
- Leading with trend words ("AI-powered", "agentic") that signal a category you have not chosen.

## Worked Example (applied to Fakhar's business)
Step 1: the clients who renew are owner-operators with 10–50 staff who were paying a VA or a junior admin to triage leads and chase documents. They compare the service to "the VA" and "the Zapier thing our nephew set up".
Step 4 (alternatives): (a) hire or keep a VA; (b) DIY with Zapier/Make plus ChatGPT; (c) do nothing, owner keeps doing it.
Step 5 (unique attributes vs those): agents that reason over unstructured messages rather than fixed triggers; monitoring and a human fallback included; built and owned by the client, not a subscription they lose; a named person accountable for outcomes; live in two weeks.
Step 6 (value themes): "Never miss a lead, including after hours" (vs VA working hours); "Nothing breaks silently" (vs DIY Zapier); "Zero owner time" (vs do nothing).
Step 7 (who cares a lot): owners whose revenue depends on fast lead response, with inbound volume high enough that a VA is already a line item.
Step 8 (frame): Big Fish, Small Pond: "AI lead-response and intake systems for multi-location clinics", rather than "AI automation agency".
Step 9 (trend): mention that patients now expect instant replies; do not lead with "AI".
Step 10: one-page document; the first line of the website becomes "Lead-response systems for clinic groups that answer, qualify and book in under five minutes, so you never pay a VA to lose a lead again."

## Caveats
- Dunford's cases are mostly B2B software; for services, "unique attributes" are often people and process, which are harder to make feel durable.
- The method assumes you have existing happy customers to study; before the first client, use interviews (07, 08) and treat the positioning as a hypothesis to test (09).
- Dunford argues against the classic positioning statement as a thinking tool; Osterwalder's ad-lib (02) is a similar template, so use it as a prototype and not as the output.
- Trends change fast in AI; a frame built on a trend ("agentic") can date within a year.
