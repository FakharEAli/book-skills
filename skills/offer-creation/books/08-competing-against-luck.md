# 08 · Competing Against Luck: The Story of Innovation and Customer Choice
**Author(s)**: Clayton M. Christensen, Taddy Hall, Karen Dillon, David S. Duncan | **Year**: 2016 | **Rank in category**: 08/10
**Why it's on the list**: the job the customer hires a product for; functional, social, emotional dimensions; job spec

## Core Idea
Customers do not buy products; they hire them to do a job, where a job is the progress a person is trying to make in a particular circumstance. Correlation data (demographics, firmographics) tells you who buys, not why; the causal mechanism is the job. Companies that understand the job, in its functional, social and emotional dimensions, can design offerings and experiences that get hired reliably instead of by luck.

## Frameworks Introduced
**The Job to Be Done (definition and dimensions)** — **How**: write the job as "progress + circumstance": "help me [progress] when [circumstance]". Then fill three dimensions: functional (what must get done), social (how I want to be seen by others), emotional (how I want to feel). Test: can a very different product be hired for the same job? If yes, you have found a job, not a product category. **Why / failure**: the failure is writing a job that is just a product description ("automate my intake"); jobs are stable across solutions, products are not.

**Where to find jobs (five sources)** — **How**: (1) jobs in your own life; (2) non-consumption: people who have no acceptable solution and so do nothing; (3) workarounds and compensating behaviours (spreadsheets, sticky notes, a nephew's Zapier); (4) things people do not want to do (negative jobs); (5) unusual uses of existing products. **Why / failure**: each is a place where the current solutions fail; surveys of current customers reveal none of them.

**The Job Spec** — **How**: a one-page document for the chosen job: the functional, emotional and social dimensions; the circumstance; the trade-offs the customer will accept; the obstacles that stop them; the experiences (in purchase and use) the solution must provide; and what "done" looks like for the customer. It is the brief for the offer. **Why / failure**: without a spec the team optimises features; with it, every deliverable is checked against the job.

**Hiring and firing; the Big Hire and Little Hire** — **How**: for every job ask what the customer is firing to hire you (a VA, a habit, doing nothing) and what would make them fire you (the little hires that fail in use). Design the purchase experience to make firing the incumbent easy and the use experience so the little hires keep succeeding. **Why / failure**: ignoring the firing side explains deals lost to "no decision".

**Three fallacies of data** — **How**: beware the fallacy of active vs passive data (once you have a product, you start measuring the product, not the job); the fallacy of surface growth (adding features for existing customers instead of nailing the job); the fallacy of conforming data (data bent to confirm the plan). **Why / failure**: keeps you from drifting from job to feature list.

## Key Principles
- Write the job before the offer, in the form "help me make [progress] when [circumstance]", because the circumstance is what makes the same owner hire different solutions.
- Include emotional and social dimensions explicitly, because the owner is also hiring you to stop feeling like the bottleneck and to look responsive to customers.
- Look for non-consumers and workarounds, because the owner duct-taping Zapier at midnight is a clearer signal of an unmet job than any survey.
- Define what the customer is firing, because the offer must make firing easy (migration, parallel running, no lock-in).
- Write the job spec and keep every deliverable accountable to it; cut what does not serve the job.
- Design the experiences around purchase and use, not only the output, because the job includes feeling safe buying and confident using.
- Resist adding features for existing clients until the core job is nailed for the segment.
- Treat job discovery as interviewing, not surveying; the story of a real hire reveals the job, a rating scale does not.

## Anti-patterns
- Segmenting by firmographics ("SMBs with 10–50 staff") and calling it a job.
- Writing the job as the product ("they need an AI agent").
- Ignoring the emotional and social dimensions as "soft".
- Measuring product usage and inferring the job from it.
- Piling on features in response to requests from current clients while the segment's core job is unsolved.

## Worked Example (applied to Fakhar's business)
Candidate job, written badly: "Clinic owners need AI intake automation." Written as a job: "Help me know that every patient enquiry is being handled well when I am not at the desk, so I can run three locations without being the one who answers the phone."
Dimensions: functional: every enquiry answered, qualified and booked, across channels, with records correct; emotional: relief from being on-call, trust that nothing is being dropped; social: the practice looks responsive and modern to patients and to the dentists I employ.
Circumstance: owner has just opened a second or third site and can no longer be physically present; a recent complaint about an unanswered enquiry triggered the first thought.
Sources checked: workarounds (a shared WhatsApp group, a receptionist forwarding emails to the owner's phone); non-consumption (owners who tried a chatbot, hated the patient experience, and went back to voicemail).
Firing: a part-time VA at one site; the habit of checking the inbox every evening.
Job spec extract: trade-offs accepted: slower on complex clinical questions as long as they are routed to a human quickly; obstacles: fear of a bot saying something clinically wrong, staff resistance; required experiences: the owner approves the agent's voice and rules before go-live; sees a daily digest; can switch to approval mode in one tap; "done" = a week passes with no enquiry the owner had to personally rescue.
Offer consequences: human-handoff rules and the approval document become core deliverables, not extras; the website chatbot is dropped; the headline speaks to "when you are not at the desk", not to "AI".

## Caveats
- Christensen's theory is explanatory and case-based (milkshakes, mattresses, housing); it is not a predictive quantitative method, and critics note jobs can be defined at any level of abstraction, which makes them hard to falsify.
- The book offers principles more than interview technique; Moesta (07), a co-developer of JTBD, supplies the timeline interview.
- Overlaps with Osterwalder's "customer jobs" (02), which uses the same vocabulary but as a listing tool; use 08 to find the one job that matters, 02 to map deliverables against it.
- For a small agency, the organisational chapters (integrating processes around the job) matter less than the discovery chapters.
