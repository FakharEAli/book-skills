## Core Frameworks & Mental Models

### 1. Find the message before you write (research)
- **Voice-of-customer message mining** (Wiebe) — the strongest copy is found in what customers already say, not invented. Mine reviews, call transcripts, support tickets and interviews; tag by pain, desired outcome, objection, "aha" moment; lift the highest-frequency, highest-emotion phrases as headline candidates. *Use before any landing page or cold-email campaign. Prefer a clumsy customer phrase over a polished agency phrase because it pattern-matches the reader's own thoughts.*
- **Mass desire; channel it, don't create it** (Schwartz) — copy cannot manufacture a desire; it can only take a desire already in millions of minds and point it at your product. *Use when a page feels "educational": ask which existing desire (more time, fewer staff headaches, not falling behind on AI) you are channelling. If you must create the desire, you have the wrong market.*
- **Do your homework** (Ogilvy) — study the product, competitors and prior research before writing a word; positioning = what the product does and who it is for. *Use before proposals: write the one-sentence positioning first.*
- **Starving crowd / market before message** (Halbert) — the list (who you reach) beats the copy. *Use when a campaign flops: suspect the audience and offer before rewriting words.*
- **Salesmanship in print** (Hopkins) — judge every line as a salesperson in a one-to-one conversation would; would you say it face to face? *Use as an edit test on every paragraph.*

### 2. Match the message to the market (strategy)
- **5 Stages of Market Awareness** (Schwartz) — Unaware → Problem Aware → Solution Aware → Product Aware → Most Aware. The stage dictates where the headline begins: Most Aware gets product + price; Product Aware gets a claim or proof; Solution Aware gets the result named and your product introduced as the way; Problem Aware gets the problem named and intensified; Unaware gets identification with who they are. *Use when choosing a headline: name the stage first.*
- **5 Stages of Market Sophistication** (Schwartz) — 1st: state the claim directly; 2nd: enlarge the claim; 3rd: introduce a new mechanism; 4th: enlarge/elaborate the mechanism; 5th: identification with the prospect, the claim is beneath notice. *AI automation in 2026 is mostly stage 3–4: "we automate your business with AI" is a dead stage-1 claim. Lead with a distinctive mechanism (how you do it) and proof.*
- **Six lead types + the Rule of One** (Masterson & Forde) — one big idea, one core emotion, one story, one benefit, one response. Direct leads for aware prospects: **Offer**, **Promise**, **Problem-Solution**. Indirect leads for less aware: **Big Secret**, **Proclamation**, **Story**. *Map: Most/Product Aware → Offer or Promise; Solution Aware → Problem-Solution; Problem Aware → Secret or Proclamation; Unaware → Story. The more aware, the more direct.*
- **Headlines select the audience** (Hopkins; Ogilvy) — a headline is a flag to the right reader, not a pun for everyone. *Prefer "For dental practices drowning in reschedules" over "Work smarter" because the right 2% stop and the wrong 98% were never going to buy.*

### 3. Make it read to the end (flow)
- **Slippery slide** (Sugarman) — every element exists to get the reader to read the first sentence; the first sentence's only job is to get the second read; momentum carries them to the offer. *Keep the first sentence under ~8 words. Never open with the product.*
- **Seeds of curiosity** (Sugarman) — end sections with a forward hook ("But that's not the surprising part."). *Use at every scroll break on a long page and before the fold in a cold email.*
- **Sell the concept, not the product** (Sugarman) — people buy the idea ("stop losing leads at 6 pm") not the thing (an n8n workflow). *Name the concept in the hero; the mechanism comes later.*
- **AIDA + write to one person** (Halbert) — Attention, Interest, Desire, Action, addressed to one named reader in plain, short-word, short-sentence English. *Draft every cold email as a letter to one specific owner; then generalise minimally.*
- **Long copy sells when it has something to say** (Ogilvy; Bly; Hopkins) — length follows the size of the decision, not fashion. *A $3k/month retainer needs proof, mechanism and objections answered; a newsletter signup needs a sentence.*

### 4. Headline and body mechanics
- **Four functions of a headline** (Bly) — get attention, select the audience, deliver a complete message, draw the reader into the body. *Score every headline candidate 0–4.*
- **Three appeals: self-interest, news, curiosity** (Caples) — self-interest wins most often; news second; curiosity alone fails. *Combine: self-interest + news ("New: ...") beats clever every time.*
- **Features → benefits → "so what?"** (Bly) — list every feature, convert each to a benefit, then ask "so what?" until you reach something the owner would pay for. *"Runs on n8n" → "no per-seat SaaS fees" → "so what?" → "the automation costs less than the admin it replaces."*
- **Specifics beat generalities** (Hopkins; Ogilvy; Sugarman) — "cuts booking admin 11 hours a week" beats "saves time". *If you lack a number, get one from a pilot before writing.*
- **Psychological triggers as checklist** (Sugarman) — involvement, proof of value, justify the purchase, authority, satisfaction conviction (a guarantee stronger than the buyer expects), urgency, exclusivity, specificity, hope. *Audit the page: which three are present, which obvious ones are missing?*
- **Life-Force 8** (Whitman) — eight biological drives (survival, food, freedom from fear/pain, companionship, comfort, superiority/winning, protecting loved ones, social approval) plus nine learned wants. *For B2B owners, lean on freedom from fear (losing clients, falling behind), superiority (beating competitors) and social approval (looking like the modern operator). Treat Whitman's 41 tactics as A/B test ideas, not laws.*
- **Test, don't argue** (Hopkins; Caples) — the only proof of a headline is a measured result. *Run two subject lines or two heroes; keep what wins; retire opinions.*
