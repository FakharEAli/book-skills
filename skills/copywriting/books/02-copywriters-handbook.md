# 02 · The Copywriter's Handbook (4th ed.)
**Author(s)**: Robert W. Bly | **Year**: 1985; 4th ed. 2020 | **Rank in category**: 02/10
**Why it's on the list**: the starting manual: headlines, features vs benefits, structure, formats, the "so what" test

## Core Idea
Copywriting is selling, and selling has learnable mechanics. Bly's claim is that a competent writer who understands the product, the audience and the objective, and who applies a handful of structural rules (headline functions, benefits over features, a motivating sequence, clear plain writing) will beat a brilliant writer who improvises. The 4th edition extends the same rules to web pages, email, landing pages and social.

## Frameworks Introduced
**Four Functions of a Headline** — every headline must (1) get attention, (2) select the audience, (3) deliver a complete message, (4) draw the reader into the body copy. **How**: score each candidate 0–4; a headline scoring under 3 is rewritten. A headline that only gets attention ("Stop!") fails selection and message. **Why**: the headline is read by most of the audience; the body by a fraction; if the headline doesn't carry a complete selling message, most readers never receive one. Failure: puns and clever lines that score 1.

**Eight Basic Headline Types** — Direct ("Automate your intake for $X/month"), Indirect (curiosity, pays off in body), News ("Introducing..."), How-To, Question, Command, Reason-Why ("7 reasons..."), Testimonial. **How**: draft one of each for the same page, then pick by the audience's awareness. **Why**: forcing eight variants breaks the first-idea habit.

**Features → Benefits and the "So What?" Test** — a feature is what the product is or has; a benefit is what the reader gets. **How**: two-column list; for every feature write the benefit; then ask "so what?" of each benefit and answer until the answer is money, time, status or safety the reader will pay for. **Why**: owners buy outcomes. Failure: stopping at a first-order benefit ("saves time") that still needs a "so what".

**The Motivating Sequence** — (1) get attention, (2) show a need, (3) satisfy the need and position the product as the solution, (4) prove the product does what you claim, (5) ask for action. **How**: use it as the skeleton for any page, email or proposal section. **Why**: it is AIDA with the proof step made explicit, which is where B2B copy usually fails.

**BFD: Beliefs, Feelings, Desires** — before writing, write down what the audience believes (about AI, about agencies, about their own staff), what they feel (fear of being left behind, suspicion of hype), and what they desire. **How**: three short lists from research; every claim must respect a belief, touch a feeling, and serve a desire. **Why**: copy that contradicts a belief is rejected before it is read.

## Key Principles
- Write the headline last-but-test-first: draft twenty, pick by the four functions, because the headline carries most of the result.
- Lead with the strongest benefit, because readers decide in the first lines whether they are the audience.
- Put specifics everywhere: numbers, names, timeframes. "Reduces invoice-chasing by 6 hours a week" beats "streamlines finance".
- Write in plain conversational English: short words, short sentences, short paragraphs, active voice, "you". Clarity is the style.
- Prove every claim with testimonials, numbers, demos, case studies or guarantees, because business buyers are paid to be sceptical.
- Length follows the decision: longer copy for high-ticket, considered purchases; shorter for low-risk actions.
- Always ask for a specific action and make it easy, because interest without a next step decays in minutes.
- Know the product better than the client's own staff before you write; most weak copy is weak research.

## Anti-patterns
- Writing to impress other marketers or win awards; the reader is a busy owner, not a judge.
- Clever-but-vague headlines that fail the "complete message" function.
- Listing features without the so-what chain (common in agency proposals).
- Burying the offer or CTA; one page, one primary action.
- "We" copy: count "we/our" vs "you/your" in a draft; if we wins, rewrite.

## Worked Example (applied to Fakhar's business)
Proposal section for an AI research agent that compiles weekly competitor and market briefs for a consultancy's partners.

Feature → benefit → so what:
- "Runs nightly on n8n, pulls 40 sources" → "Partners get a fresh brief every Monday 7 am" → so what? → "They walk into client calls with yesterday's news, not last quarter's."
- "Cites every claim with a link" → "No one has to double-check it" → so what? → "A junior analyst's 6 hours a week goes back to billable work."
- "Custom-built, no per-seat licence" → "Cost doesn't grow with headcount" → so what? → "Flat fee is below one day of analyst time a month."

Motivating sequence applied to the proposal:
1. Attention (headline): *"Your partners' Monday briefs, researched overnight, cited, and costing less than one analyst-day a month."*
2. Need: quote their own words from discovery ("we find out about competitor moves from clients").
3. Satisfy/position: how the agent works in three steps, one paragraph.
4. Proof: pilot result, sample brief attached, one named testimonial or a specific number from a similar build.
5. Action: "Reply 'go' and the first brief lands next Monday; 30-day exit if it isn't useful."

Headline variants to test by type: How-To ("How three-partner firms get analyst-grade briefs without an analyst"), Reason-Why, Question ("What did your competitors announce last week?").

## Caveats
- A generalist manual; it does not go deep on awareness/sophistication (01) or message research (08); use it for mechanics.
- Some 4th-edition digital advice (SEO, early social) ages quickly; the headline and benefit rules do not.
- Bly favours long copy; Sugarman (03) agrees, Wiebe (08) would say length is decided by the research, not by rule.
