# 06 · Great Leads: The Six Easiest Ways to Start Any Sales Message
**Author(s)**: Michael Masterson (Mark Ford), John Forde | **Year**: 2011 | **Rank in category**: 06/10
**Why it's on the list**: the Rule of One; six lead types (offer, promise, problem-solution, big secret, proclamation, story) mapped to awareness

## Core Idea
The lead, the opening stretch of a sales message (roughly the first 10–20%, up to the first few hundred words in a long letter), decides whether the rest gets read; most failed promotions failed in the lead. There are only six workable ways to open, they sit on a spectrum from direct to indirect, and the right one is dictated by how aware the prospect already is (Schwartz's stages). Pick the lead by the reader, then obey the Rule of One.

## Frameworks Introduced
**The Rule of One** — a strong sales message has one big idea, one core emotion, one captivating story, one single desirable benefit, and one inevitable response. **How**: before drafting, fill five lines: Idea / Emotion / Story / Benefit / Response. If a line has two entries, cut one. Everything in the lead serves those five. **Why**: multiple ideas dilute; one idea repeated from different angles is remembered and acted on. Failure: the "and also" lead that lists everything the service does.

**The Six Lead Types** (direct → indirect):
1. **Offer Lead** — opens with the deal itself (product, price, terms, bonus). For Most Aware readers. "Your first 30 days of AI follow-up free; cancel by text."
2. **Promise Lead** — opens with the biggest single benefit, boldly stated. For Product/Solution Aware. "Every missed call booked in 90 seconds."
3. **Problem-Solution Lead** — names the pain the reader feels, agitates it, then presents the solution. For Solution/Problem Aware. "Half your after-hours calls go to voicemail. Here is where those jobs go."
4. **Big Secret Lead** — promises a hidden insight or method, withholds it to pull the reader in; the product is the vehicle for the secret. For Problem Aware. "The scheduling trick a 9-truck HVAC firm uses to run without a dispatcher."
5. **Proclamation Lead** — a bold, surprising or contrarian statement, often with a prediction, that demands explanation. For Problem Aware/Unaware. "By 2027 the receptionist role in trades businesses will not exist."
6. **Story Lead** — opens with a narrative the reader identifies with; the sale emerges from the story. For Unaware. "A paralegal messaged me at 11:40 pm."

**How to choose**: identify the awareness stage; the more aware, the more direct; indirect leads buy attention from readers who would reject a direct claim. **Why it works**: a direct lead to an unaware reader is noise; an indirect lead to an aware reader is an irritating delay. Failure: using Story or Secret because they are fun to write when the traffic already knows what it wants.

**Direct vs Indirect** — direct leads name the product or benefit early and are judged on clarity; indirect leads withhold and are judged on intrigue plus payoff. Indirect leads must pay off: the secret must be real, the proclamation must be defended, the story must land on the product.

## Key Principles
- Decide the awareness stage first, then the lead type, because the lead is for the reader, not for you.
- Write the Rule of One sheet before the lead, because a lead with two ideas is two weak leads.
- Make the lead carry the whole emotional argument; the body supplies proof and detail.
- Pay off every indirect lead explicitly, because an unpaid promise poisons the next read.
- For cold channels (LinkedIn, cold email) default to indirect or Problem-Solution; for retargeting and warm lists default to Promise or Offer.
- Prefer Problem-Solution when in doubt for B2B; it is the lead that works across the middle of the awareness spectrum.
- Rewrite the lead more than the body; a strong body cannot rescue a weak lead.

## Anti-patterns
- Opening with "In today's fast-paced world..." (no lead type, no reader).
- Combining lead types ("a secret story about an offer").
- Story leads that are about you, not a reader the prospect recognises.
- Proclamations you cannot defend in the next paragraph.
- Offer leads to cold traffic (the deal means nothing to someone who doesn't want the thing).

## Worked Example (applied to Fakhar's business)
Same service (AI follow-up for trades businesses), one Rule of One sheet, three leads for three traffic sources.

Rule of One: Idea = missed calls are lost jobs you can recover automatically; Emotion = frustration at money leaking through voicemail; Story = the Saturday 7 pm call that became a Monday job; Benefit = recovered revenue without hiring; Response = book a demo on their own missed calls.

- Cold LinkedIn post (Problem Aware) → **Proclamation**: *"The most expensive employee in most trades businesses is the voicemail greeting."* Then defend: numbers on unanswered calls, what callers do next (ring the next company), the recovered-job figure.
- Google Ads landing page for "AI receptionist for plumbers" (Solution Aware) → **Problem-Solution**: *"You already know you miss calls. Here is what happens to each one now: texted back in 90 seconds, quoted from your price book, booked into your calendar."*
- Retargeting email to pricing-page visitors (Most Aware) → **Offer**: *"First 30 days free, set up in a week, cancel by replying 'stop'. Shall I start Monday?"*

Same idea, same emotion, same story available in each, different door for a different reader.

## Caveats
- Written for long-form financial and health direct mail at Agora; those leads run thousands of words, so scale the lead to the medium (first two lines of a post, first screen of a page, first sentence of an email).
- The awareness mapping is a heuristic the authors borrow from Schwartz; verify the stage with research (08) rather than assuming.
- Indirect leads can tip into clickbait on LinkedIn; the payoff standard is higher there because readers have been burned.
