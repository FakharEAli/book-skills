# 08 · Where Stellar Messages Come From
**Author(s)**: Joanna Wiebe (Copyhackers) | **Year**: 2024 | **Rank in category**: 08/10
**Why it's on the list**: voice-of-customer research, message mining from reviews/interviews, finding the message before writing

**Confidence note**: this file is built from Wiebe's publicly taught conversion-copywriting method (Copyhackers), which the book formalises. Framework names below are those she uses in that body of work; chapter-level specifics of the book itself are not reproduced.

## Core Idea
Copy is not written; the message is found, and the copy is the message's delivery. The strongest message on a page is almost always something a customer already said, in their words, about the pain they had, the result they got, or the moment they realised the product was different. The copywriter's first job is research: mine reviews, interviews, surveys, support tickets and sales calls for that language; the second job is to choose and organise the message; writing sentences comes third and is the easiest part.

## Frameworks Introduced
**Voice-of-Customer (VOC) Research** — systematic collection of customers' own words. **How**: (1) gather sources: your own sales-call transcripts, onboarding calls, client emails, review sites for competitors and adjacent tools (G2, Capterra, Trustpilot, Amazon for books on the topic), Reddit/forum threads, survey open-text; (2) read everything and copy out verbatim phrases (never paraphrase at this stage); (3) tag each phrase: pain, desired outcome, objection/anxiety, trigger event ("what made you look"), language about alternatives, "aha" moment; (4) count frequency and rate intensity (emotion words, specifics); (5) the high-frequency, high-intensity phrases are your message candidates. **Why**: customers pattern-match copy to their own thoughts; borrowed language feels true, agency language feels like marketing. Failure: paraphrasing during collection, which launders the emotion out.

**Message Mining (review mining)** — the VOC method applied to reviews of competitors and substitutes. **How**: for an AI-automation offer, mine reviews of Zapier/Make, VA agencies, scheduling tools, CRMs, and "AI receptionist" products; one-star and five-star reviews are richest; capture what people loved, what broke, what they wish existed. **Why**: competitors' customers have already articulated the gap you fill. Failure: mining only your own happy clients.

**Message before copy (the "one message" discipline)** — before drafting, decide the single message the page must land, the one thing the reader should believe after reading. **How**: write it as a sentence a customer could have said; check it against awareness stage (01); every section either supports it or is cut. **Why**: pages fail from message sprawl more than from weak sentences. Failure: a "value proposition" written by committee that no customer would ever utter.

**Sticky messaging via the "sweet spot"** — the message that sits where three things overlap: what customers say they want most, what the product does best, and what competitors cannot or do not say. **How**: three lists; the intersection is the hero. **Why**: desirable + true + different.

**Research → Message → Draft → Validate → Optimise** — the conversion-copywriting process. **How**: research (VOC, product, competitors, analytics); define the message hierarchy (what goes first, second, third, by what the reader needs to believe in order); draft fast; validate with real prospects (five-second test, read-aloud, "what would you do next") and with data; optimise by testing.

## Key Principles
- Spend more than half your time in research, because the message is found there, not at the keyboard.
- Collect verbatim, because the customer's clumsy phrase ("it just runs, I forget it's there") beats your polished one.
- Mine competitors' negative reviews, because they describe the job your offer must do.
- Decide one message per page and write it as a customer sentence, because sprawl kills conversion.
- Order the page by what the reader must believe next, not by what you want to say first.
- Headline candidates come from the mined phrases, not from brainstorming.
- Validate with prospects before launch, because you are not the reader.
- Keep a swipe file of mined phrases by segment; it compounds across campaigns.

## Anti-patterns
- Writing the hero before talking to or reading a single customer.
- Paraphrasing interviews into marketing-speak.
- Treating a tagline as a message.
- Mining only testimonials (filtered praise), ignoring complaints and trigger events.
- Letting the client's internal vocabulary ("solutions", "synergies") replace the customer's.

## Worked Example (applied to Fakhar's business)
Landing page for AI appointment-reminder and reschedule automation for dental practices.

Research sources: 6 discovery-call transcripts with practice managers; 180 reviews of two dental-reminder SaaS tools (1- and 5-star first); 2 Reddit threads on front-desk burnout; the client's own cancellation data.

Mined verbatim phrases (tagged):
- Pain: "we spend the whole morning ringing people who don't pick up" (×7 variants); "no-shows are killing Fridays" (×4)
- Desired outcome: "I just want the chair filled" (×5); "stop making my front desk chase"
- Objection: "the last system texted patients at 3 am" (×3); "patients hated the robot voice"
- Trigger: "we lost two receptionists in a year"
- Aha (from competitor 5-star): "it filled the gap from a cancellation before I even noticed"

One message (customer sentence): "It fills the cancelled chair before I notice it was empty."

Sweet spot: customers want filled chairs without chasing; your build re-offers cancelled slots to a waitlist by text within minutes and never messages outside practice hours; competitor reviews complain about blind blasts and night-time texts.

Hero (Solution Aware, Problem-Solution lead): *"When a patient cancels, the chair is re-offered to your waitlist by text in 4 minutes. Your front desk never rings a no-show again."* Subhead: *"Sends only in practice hours. Sounds like your practice, not a robot."* Section order by belief: it works (demo clip) → it won't annoy patients (quiet hours, tone samples) → it fits our software (integrations) → what it costs vs one no-show a day → what if it breaks → book.

## Caveats
- The method assumes you have, or can get, customer data; for a brand-new offer, mine adjacent markets and run five interviews before writing.
- VOC tells you what people say; Schwartz (01) warns that what they say and what moves them can differ; weight phrases by emotion and by purchase-adjacent context, not just frequency.
- Review mining is public data but respect platform terms; summarise and anonymise phrases you publish.
