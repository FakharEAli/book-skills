# 01 · Breakthrough Advertising
**Author(s)**: Eugene M. Schwartz | **Year**: 1966 | **Rank in category**: 01/10
**Why it's on the list**: mass desire, 5 stages of market awareness, 5 stages of market sophistication, channeling desire not creating it

## Core Idea
Advertising cannot create desire. The desires that move people (to be richer, safer, admired, less tired) already exist in millions of minds; the copywriter's whole job is to find the strongest existing desire, aim it at a product, and make the product its obvious fulfilment. Which words do that depends on two things about the market, not about you: how aware the prospect is of the problem and your product, and how many similar promises they have already heard.

## Frameworks Introduced
**Mass Desire** — the public spread of a private want. **How**: evaluate a desire on three dimensions before building a campaign on it: urgency/intensity (how badly they want it now), staying power/repetition (does it recur or fade after one purchase), and scope (how many people share it). Pick the desire that scores highest across all three that your product can credibly fulfil, then make the headline the point where that desire meets the product. **Why it works / failure mode**: it keeps you from "educating" a market that doesn't care. Failure: trying to sell the mechanism (AI, workflows) when the desire is "stop doing payroll at midnight".

**5 Stages of Market Awareness** — where the prospect's mind is when your headline arrives. **How**: (1) Most Aware: knows your product, wants it, needs only the deal; headline is product + price/offer. (2) Product Aware: knows your product, not yet convinced; headline reinforces the claim, adds proof, sharpens the mechanism or extends the promise. (3) Solution Aware: knows the result they want, doesn't know you deliver it; headline names the desire, then introduces the product as the way. (4) Problem Aware: feels the problem, doesn't know a solution exists; headline names and intensifies the problem, then reveals a solution. (5) Unaware: no felt problem; headline must first identify with who they are or what they feel, with no product and no claim up front. **Why**: a headline that starts ahead of the reader's awareness is gibberish; one that starts behind it is boring. Failure: writing stage-1 headlines (your product name, your price) to stage-4 traffic from a LinkedIn post.

**5 Stages of Market Sophistication** — how many times the prospect has heard promises like yours. **How**: (1) First to market: state the claim simply and directly. (2) Competitors appear: enlarge the claim (faster, more, cheaper). (3) Claims exhausted: introduce a new mechanism, a how-it-works that makes the old promise believable again. (4) Mechanisms compete: elaborate and enlarge the mechanism (easier, more complete, works where others failed). (5) Market disbelieves everything: drop the claim to the background; lead with identification, the prospect's own identity and feelings. **Why**: belief, not desire, is the bottleneck in a sophisticated market. Failure: shouting a bigger version of a dead claim.

**Intensification, Identification, Gradualization, Mechanization, Concentration, Camouflage, Redefinition** — Schwartz's body-copy techniques. Intensification piles up concrete images of the desire fulfilled; Identification links the product to a role the prospect wants to be seen in; Gradualization walks a sceptical reader from accepted facts to your claim in small steps; Mechanization explains the how to earn belief; Concentration attacks competitors' weak points; Camouflage borrows the authority of a trusted format; Redefinition reframes what the product category is. Use one dominant technique per piece.

## Key Principles
- Decide the awareness stage before writing the headline, because the stage dictates which word comes first (product, claim, result, problem, or the reader).
- Decide the sophistication stage before writing the claim, because a stage-1 claim in a stage-4 market is read as a lie.
- Lead with mechanism when competitors all promise the same outcome, because a new "how" revives belief in an old "what".
- Spend research time on the market's existing language and desires, not on your product, because the product is only the channel.
- Intensify desire with specific pictures of the outcome, not adjectives, because images are felt and adjectives are discounted.
- Use gradualization with sceptical operators: start from facts they already accept ("you already lose Friday afternoons to invoicing") before the claim.
- Sell the state the prospect wants to be in, not the thing you ship.
- Change the headline, not the market: when the market moves a stage, your copy must move with it or die.

## Anti-patterns
- Trying to create desire with information. People don't become hungry because you describe food.
- Clever or curiosity-only headlines to an aware market; they want the deal, give it to them.
- Leading with price or product name to a problem-aware market; they don't know why they should care yet.
- Enlarging a worn-out claim ("10x your productivity") in a market that has heard it from everyone.

## Worked Example (applied to Fakhar's business)
Service: AI-powered lead follow-up for home-services businesses (plumbers, HVAC, roofers). Market research says: owners know missed calls cost jobs (Problem Aware → Solution Aware), and they've seen "AI receptionist" ads everywhere (sophistication stage 3 → 4).

Awareness diagnosis: Solution Aware. They want "never miss a job", they don't know you. Sophistication: stage 4; "AI answers your calls" is a worn mechanism.

Hero built from the diagnosis:
- Headline (names the desire, then the product as the way, with an elaborated mechanism): *"Every missed call texted back, quoted and booked in under 90 seconds, by an assistant trained on your past jobs, not a generic script."*
- Sub-head (gradualization): *"You already lose 1 in 4 after-hours calls to voicemail. Here's what happens to those calls now."*
- Body: Mechanization (how it reads the CRM, how it quotes), Intensification (Saturday 7 pm call becomes a Monday 8 am job on the board), then Concentration (why script-based AI receptionists get hung up on).
- CTA matches awareness: not "Buy now" but "See it handle one of your real missed calls".

For a Most Aware retargeting audience (visited pricing twice), swap the hero to Offer: *"$X/month, first 30 days free, cancel by text."*

## Caveats
- Written for 1960s mail-order and print; Schwartz's examples (hair tonics, courses, cars) need wholesale translation; the stage logic transfers intact.
- Schwartz offers no measurement method for diagnosing a stage; combine with Wiebe's message mining (08) and Hopkins/Caples testing (04, 07) to verify your guess.
- The five-stage sophistication curve is a model, not a law; markets fragment, and a niche (e.g., dental practices) can sit at stage 2 while the broad "AI agency" market sits at stage 4.
