# 04 · Scientific Advertising
**Author(s)**: Claude C. Hopkins | **Year**: 1923 | **Rank in category**: 04/10
**Why it's on the list**: specific claims, salesmanship in print, testing, free samples, headlines select the audience

## Core Idea
Advertising is salesmanship, multiplied, and should be judged by a salesperson's standard: did it sell, measurably? Hopkins learned from mail-order and keyed coupons that every claim can be tested, that specific statements outsell general ones, that the headline's job is to pick out the buyer, and that offering service or a sample beats boasting. Opinions about copy are worthless next to a counted result.

## Frameworks Introduced
**Salesmanship in Print** — the test for every line: would a good salesperson say this to one prospect across a desk? **How**: read the draft aloud as a conversation; delete anything you would be embarrassed to say face to face (boasts, superlatives, jokes at the prospect's expense, "we are the leading..."). **Why**: readers are individuals, not a crowd; copy that performs for an audience fails the person. Failure: writing to entertain or to impress colleagues.

**Specific Claims** — generalities ("best", "fastest") are discounted as puffery; specifics ("cuts admin 6.5 hours a week") are read as fact and invite the reader to check. **How**: replace every adjective with a measurable; if you have no measure, get one before publishing. **Why**: a specific statement implies you can prove it; a general one implies you can't. Failure: inventing precision you cannot back.

**Headlines Select** — a headline is a signal to the right reader, not a greeting to everyone. **How**: name the reader or their situation in the headline ("To the owner doing invoicing on Sunday"); accept that most people scroll past. **Why**: the cost of a non-buyer ignoring you is zero; the cost of a buyer not recognising themselves is the sale.

**Test with Keyed Responses** — run two versions, each with its own tracking, and count. **How**: change one variable (headline, offer, lead), split traffic or list, measure replies, demos booked, revenue, not clicks. Keep the winner, test the next variable. **Why**: it replaces debate with data. Failure: testing with samples too small to mean anything, or testing colour and font before headline and offer.

**Samples and Service** — offer a trial, a demonstration, or a piece of genuine help rather than asking for trust. **How**: structure the first step as low-risk and self-evidently useful: a free audit, a worked example on their own data, a two-week pilot. **Why**: people act on what they can see; they resist being sold. Failure: "free" offers that are disguised sales pitches, which Hopkins says destroy trust.

**Tell the Full Story** — the reader who is interested wants everything; give it in one place because you may never get them back. **How**: long, complete copy on the page where the decision is made; short copy only where the action is trivial.

## Key Principles
- Count results and let the count decide, because experts' opinions about copy are wrong about half the time.
- State the specific, because specifics are believed and generalities are skimmed.
- Offer service, not boasts: show what the reader gets, never what you are.
- Use the headline to sort readers, because reaching the wrong people cheaply is still waste.
- Give the complete argument where the decision happens; the interested reader will read it.
- Make the first step a sample: a demo on their data, not a pitch about yours.
- Never try to be funny or clever at the expense of clarity; buying is a serious act.
- Study the prospect's own habits and language; advertising is applied human nature.

## Anti-patterns
- Boasting ("industry-leading", "cutting-edge AI"); it signals you have nothing specific to say.
- Entertaining instead of selling.
- Negative or scolding copy; show the bright side the reader gets.
- Changing copy on a hunch when a test could settle it.
- Asking for a big commitment before offering a small proof.

## Worked Example (applied to Fakhar's business)
Cold outreach to e-commerce owners for an AI customer-service triage build.

Before (boasting): "We're a leading AI automation agency helping brands transform support with cutting-edge AI."

After (specific, selecting, service-first):
> Subject: your Shopify tickets after 6 pm
>
> Hi [Name],
>
> For a store doing roughly your volume, about 40% of support tickets are "where is my order". Those can be answered from the tracking number in under 10 seconds, automatically.
>
> I'd like to prove that on your own tickets, not on a slide. Export last week's tickets, I'll run them through the triage assistant we built for [similar store type], and send you back the sorted results and the answers it would have sent. Takes you five minutes, costs nothing, and you keep the analysis either way.
>
> Worth a look?

Test plan (Hopkins): two subject lines ("your Shopify tickets after 6 pm" vs "40% of your tickets answer themselves") to equal lists, measured by replies that accept the sample, not by opens. Keep the winner and test the offer next (free triage of one week vs free triage of one day).

## Caveats
- 1923: coupons, newspapers, soap and cereal. Every tactic needs translation; the logic (specifics, selection, samples, counting) is unchanged.
- Hopkins' "people won't be bored" and "never joke" are stated as absolutes; social feeds reward a lighter register, so treat them as defaults for sales pages and emails, not for all content.
- He under-weights brand and long-term reputation (Ogilvy, 05, corrects this).
