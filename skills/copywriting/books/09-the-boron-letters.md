# 09 · The Boron Letters
**Author(s)**: Gary C. Halbert (with commentary by Bond Halbert) | **Year**: letters written 1984; published as a book 2013 | **Rank in category**: 09/10
**Why it's on the list**: starving crowd, A-pile mail, AIDA, write to one person, market selection over copy

## Core Idea
Letters a direct-mail copywriter wrote from prison to his teenage son, teaching the whole trade in order of importance: first pick a market that is already hungry, then get your message physically opened and read, then write like one person talking to another in the plainest possible words, then structure it so attention becomes action. The words matter least of the four; the market matters most. Half the book is about taking care of your body and mind because the writer is the instrument.

## Frameworks Introduced
**The Starving Crowd** — asked what advantage he would want when opening a hamburger stand, Halbert's answer is not location, quality or price: it is a starving crowd. **How**: before writing anything, qualify the market: do they already want this badly? have they recently bought related things? can you reach them? **Why**: copy aimed at a hungry market converts despite flaws; copy aimed at an indifferent one fails despite brilliance. Failure: spending weeks polishing a page for a market that hasn't felt the problem.

**Market before message: the list** — in direct mail, the list (who receives it) outranks the offer, which outranks the copy. **How**: Halbert's buyer-list criteria translate directly: recency (bought something related lately), frequency (buys repeatedly), monetary (spends real money), and relevance (bought something like yours, by the same channel). For Fakhar: target owners who recently hired, recently bought software, or recently posted about being overwhelmed, not "all SMBs". **Why**: proven buyers buy again; cold demographics do not.

**A-pile vs B-pile** — people sort mail into the A-pile (personal, must open) and the B-pile (obviously commercial, often binned unopened). **How**: make the message look personal: in mail, a hand-addressed envelope and a real stamp; in email, a plain-text message, a subject line a colleague would write, no HTML template, no image header, a first line about them; on LinkedIn, a DM that references something specific they said. **Why**: nothing you write matters if it is never opened. Failure: designed emails with logos and "unsubscribe" footers that scream B-pile.

**AIDA** — Attention, Interest, Desire, Action, Halbert's structure for the letter after it is opened. **How**: Attention: a first line that is about them and surprising; Interest: facts and a story relevant to their situation; Desire: paint the outcome in concrete pictures and proof; Action: tell them exactly what to do next, once, simply. **Why**: a letter that is all attention never sells; one that is all action is never read.

**Write to one person, in plain words** — picture one named reader and write as if talking to them. **How**: short words, short sentences, short paragraphs; "eye relief" (white space, indents, subheads) so the page looks easy; conversational transitions ("Here's why.", "Now listen."); read it aloud; cut anything you would not say. Copy great ads out by hand to internalise rhythm. **Why**: the reader is alone with your page; crowd-speak feels like a broadcast.

## Key Principles
- Pick the crowd first, because a hungry market forgives weak copy and an indifferent one forgives nothing.
- Qualify prospects by recent behaviour (hired, bought, complained), because recency predicts buying.
- Make the message look personal, because the A-pile is the only pile that gets read.
- Open with them, not you, because attention is granted to relevance.
- Write to one person in the simplest words, because clarity is persuasion.
- Give the eye relief: short paragraphs, white space, subheads, because a dense page is unread.
- Ask for one specific action, because two asks produce none.
- Study what already works (keep a file of winning emails, posts, pages) and copy structure, not words.
- Protect the writer: sleep, exercise, routine; Halbert treats this as part of the method.

## Anti-patterns
- Writing before choosing the market ("we help businesses...").
- Branded, designed cold emails; they announce themselves as pitches.
- Long opening paragraphs about your agency.
- Big words and jargon to sound expert; it reads as distance.
- Multiple CTAs; "book a call, or download, or reply, or follow".

## Worked Example (applied to Fakhar's business)
Cold email, written to one person, A-pile style.

Market selection (starving crowd): owners of recruitment agencies with 5–20 recruiters who posted a job for a "recruitment coordinator/admin" in the last 60 days (they are feeling the admin load right now and are about to spend money on it).

> Subject: the coordinator role you posted
>
> Hi Priya,
>
> Saw the coordinator ad on your site. Before you hire, one thought.
>
> Most of what that role does at a 12-recruiter firm is formatting CVs, chasing candidates for availability, and updating the ATS. We built an assistant for [a similar agency] that does those three things, inside their existing ATS. It took their consultants' admin from about 9 hours a week each to under 2.
>
> It costs less than a quarter of a coordinator's salary and doesn't resign.
>
> If you want, send me three anonymised CVs you'd normally reformat and I'll send them back done the way your templates want, so you can see it on your own work.
>
> Fakhar

AIDA check: Attention (their own job ad), Interest (what the role really does), Desire (hours, cost, "doesn't resign"), Action (one ask: send three CVs). A-pile check: plain text, no logo, subject a colleague could have written, first line about her.

## Caveats
- The letters are 1984 direct mail; hand-addressed envelopes and live stamps become plain-text emails and personal DMs; the sorting psychology is identical.
- Halbert's list criteria are mail-order wisdom, not peer-reviewed; they align with behavioural targeting practice.
- His tone is blunt and some advice is personal (diet, exercise); keep the professional method, skip the lifestyle unless useful.
