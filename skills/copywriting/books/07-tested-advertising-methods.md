# 07 · Tested Advertising Methods
**Author(s)**: John Caples (5th ed. revised by Fred E. Hahn) | **Year**: 1932; 5th ed. 1997 | **Rank in category**: 07/10
**Why it's on the list**: headline formulas, self-interest/news/curiosity appeals, testing discipline

## Core Idea
The headline is most of the ad; the difference between a good and a bad headline, measured by keyed coupon returns, is not 10% but often several hundred percent. Caples, a mail-order copywriter who wrote "They Laughed When I Sat Down at the Piano", spent a career counting which headlines pulled, and reduced the findings to appeals, formulas and a testing discipline: appeal to self-interest, add news, use curiosity only as a booster, and let split-run tests, not taste, decide.

## Frameworks Introduced
**Three Primary Appeals** — (1) Self-interest: the headline offers the reader something they want (more money, less work, safety); (2) News: announces something new (a product, a method, a result); (3) Curiosity: provokes a question the body answers. **How**: rank candidates by appeal; a self-interest headline usually beats a news headline, which beats curiosity; the strongest combine self-interest with news. Curiosity alone, with no self-interest, is the most common failure. **Why**: readers scan for "what's in it for me"; news earns a second look; curiosity without a stake is ignored.

**Headline Formulas** — Caples catalogues dozens of tested openings; the durable set: begin with "Introducing"/"Announcing"/"New"/"Now"/"At last" (news); "How to..." and "How I..."; "Why..."; "Which..."; "Who else wants..."; "Wanted..."; "This..."; "Because..."; "If you..."; "Advice to..."; question headlines ("Do you make these mistakes in...?"); testimonial-style headlines in the first person; headlines that name a specific audience ("To the owner who..."). **How**: generate twenty headlines by running your core benefit through fifteen formulas, then shortlist by the three appeals. **Why**: formulas encode decades of counted results; they are starting points, not finished lines.

**Split-Run Testing** — run two versions to matched audiences, each keyed, and count responses. **How**: test the headline first (largest effect), then the offer, then the lead, then everything else; one variable per test; a sample large enough that the difference is not noise; measure the action you sell, not attention. **Why**: expert judgement about headlines is unreliable; Caples repeatedly reports experts picking the loser.

**Positive over negative, you over we, specific over vague** — tested tendencies: headlines that promise a gain outperform those that threaten a loss in most categories (though fear works for insurance-type products); "you" headlines outpull "we"; a number beats an adjective.

## Key Principles
- Spend most of your writing time on the headline, because it determines whether anything else is read.
- Put self-interest in the headline, because the reader asks "what do I get" before anything else.
- Add news where it is true, because "new" is among the most reliable words in tested headlines.
- Use curiosity only on top of self-interest, because a bare riddle is skipped.
- Use "you" and name the reader's situation, because selection and self-interest compound.
- Prefer a specific number to any adjective, because specifics are believed.
- Test, then believe the count over your opinion, because experts pick the losing headline about as often as the winner.
- Long copy beats short when the reader is interested; test length too.

## Anti-patterns
- Headlines that are clever, punning, or "blind" (reader cannot tell what is being sold).
- Negative headlines that insult or scold the reader.
- Testing trivial variables before the headline and offer.
- Trusting a single small test; verify winners.
- Humour in sales copy; Caples found it rarely pulled.

## Worked Example (applied to Fakhar's business)
Subject lines and hero headlines for an AI proposal-drafting assistant sold to agency owners (marketing, design, consulting).

Core benefit: proposals written in 20 minutes instead of 4 hours, in the agency's own voice.

Formula run (fifteen in two minutes):
- How to: "How to send a proposal the same afternoon as the discovery call"
- Who else: "Who else wants proposals out in 20 minutes?"
- News: "New: a proposal writer trained on your past wins"
- Question: "Do you still write proposals by hand?"
- Specific audience: "To the agency owner writing proposals at 10 pm"
- Which: "Which of your last 10 proposals took under an hour?"
- Why: "Why your best proposals take 4 hours (and how 20 minutes is enough)"
- Because: "Because the second proposal shouldn't take as long as the first"
- Testimonial-style: "'I sent it before the client got back to the office.'"

Appeal ranking: self-interest + news → "New: proposals in 20 minutes, written in your voice from your past wins" (A). Self-interest + selection → "To the agency owner writing proposals at 10 pm" (B). Curiosity-heavy → "Which of your last 10 proposals took under an hour?" (hold for a later test).

Test: A vs B as email subject lines to two halves of the same list; metric = demo bookings, not opens. Winner becomes the landing-page hero; loser's best idea becomes the subhead.

## Caveats
- Print-era coupon data; the direction of findings (self-interest, specifics, "you") has replicated widely in email and ads, the magnitudes have not been re-measured.
- Caples' dislike of humour and negative headlines is category-dependent; LinkedIn posts and fear-based B2B (compliance, churn) can break the rule, so test.
- Formulas are easy to over-apply; a headline that sounds like a formula is a stage-4/5 problem (Schwartz, 01).
