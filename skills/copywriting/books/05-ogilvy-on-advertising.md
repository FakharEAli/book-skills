# 05 · Ogilvy on Advertising
**Author(s)**: David Ogilvy | **Year**: 1983 | **Rank in category**: 05/10
**Why it's on the list**: research first, big idea, headlines read 5× body, long copy sells, positioning

## Core Idea
Advertising's purpose is to sell, and the surest route is unglamorous: do the homework (product, competitors, consumer research), decide the positioning (what the product does and who it is for), find one big idea that will last, then write factual, specific, long copy under a headline that promises a benefit. Ogilvy was Hopkins' disciple with a brand conscience: every piece should sell now and also build the brand's image for later.

## Frameworks Introduced
**Do Your Homework** — before writing, study the product until you find a fact worth a headline; study competitors' advertising; study the consumer's language and motivations through research. **How**: (1) read everything about the product, interview the people who make and use it; (2) collect competitor claims and find the unclaimed space; (3) list the words customers use; (4) only then write. **Why**: Ogilvy's famous ads came from facts found in research (an engineer's remark, a technical spec), not from invention. Failure: brainstorming headlines before you know the product.

**Positioning** — decide what the product does and who it is for; one decision, made before the copy. **How**: one sentence: "[Product] is the [category] for [who] that [does what]." Test it against competitors: is the slot empty? Is it true? Is it wanted? **Why**: a product positioned for everyone is for no one. Failure: positioning by adjective ("premium", "innovative") rather than by job and audience.

**The Big Idea** — a single creative idea strong enough to run for years. **How**: Ogilvy's screening questions: did it make me gasp with envy when I first saw it? do I wish I had thought of it? is it unique? does it fit the strategy perfectly? could it run for thirty years? **Why**: without a big idea the campaign passes like a ship in the night. Failure: confusing a clever line with an idea.

**Headline Rules** — on average about five times as many people read the headline as the body, so the headline must carry a promise; include the brand name where possible; news headlines pull; specific beats vague; avoid tricky, punning or negative headlines; write a headline that makes readers want to read on. **How**: write the promise first, add news or specificity, check it still reads as English.

**Long Copy and Layout Anatomy** — long, factual copy sells considered purchases; captions under images are read more than body copy, so make them sell; readers follow a known path (picture → headline → caption → body), so lay it out in that order; use testimonials, specifics, and a clear offer.

## Key Principles
- Research before you write, because the fact that sells is usually sitting in the product or in the customer's mouth.
- Position first: one sentence on who and what, because the copy is only the elaboration.
- Put the promise in the headline, because that is the only line most people read.
- Prefer long, factual copy for a considered purchase, because the interested reader wants all the facts and the uninterested one wasn't going to buy anyway.
- Make captions and subheads carry the selling argument, because skimmers read only those.
- Be specific and factual; avoid superlatives and generalities, because the reader is intelligent and discounts puffery.
- Write the way people talk; do not be clever or literary.
- Every ad should contribute to the brand image, because tactical wins that cheapen the brand cost more later.

## Anti-patterns
- Headlines that are puns, tricks or in-jokes.
- Copy written to win awards or impress peers.
- Committees writing by consensus; one author, one idea.
- Changing a working campaign because the team is bored before the market is.
- Treating the reader as a fool: condescension, hype, shouting.

## Worked Example (applied to Fakhar's business)
Agency website homepage.

Homework findings: competitors say "AI automation agency", "we build AI agents", "transform your business"; none says who for; client interviews repeat "I don't want another tool, I want the thing to just run" and "I can't tell what's hype".

Positioning sentence: "[Agency] is the done-for-you automation partner for owner-run service businesses (10–60 staff) that replaces a specific admin role's workload within 60 days, built so it keeps running without you."

Big idea candidate: "Hire the workflow, not the headcount." Screen: unique against competitors (yes); fits strategy (yes); durable (yes, as long as hiring is expensive); makes a competitor envious (plausibly).

Hero:
- Headline (promise + specificity): *"Replace one admin role's workload in 60 days, with automations that keep running when you stop watching."*
- Caption under the hero screenshot (sells, not describes): *"The Monday brief this agent wrote for a 14-person consultancy at 6:12 am. Nobody was awake."*
- Body: long and factual; three named builds with numbers, how the 60-day process works, what happens if it breaks, pricing logic, who it is not for.
- Brand: every page uses the same plain, confident register; no "cutting-edge", no exclamation marks.

## Caveats
- 1983, print and TV; the five-times headline ratio and layout path are print research and should be treated as directional for web, where eye-tracking differs.
- Ogilvy's research base is Gallup-era and his own agency's data; cite as practitioner evidence.
- His preference for long copy assumes an interested reader on the page; for cold channels (LinkedIn, email), Halbert (09) and Sugarman (03) are the better guides to earning that interest first.
