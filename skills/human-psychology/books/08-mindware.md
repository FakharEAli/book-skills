# 08 · Mindware: Tools for Smart Thinking
**Author(s)**: Richard E. Nisbett | **Year**: 2015 | **Rank in category**: 08/10
**Why it's on the list**: practical reasoning tools for judging evidence and avoiding bad conclusions

## Core Idea
Good reasoning is a set of learnable tools, and most of them are statistical and scientific habits applied to everyday problems: see the situation not just the person, respect sample size, distinguish correlation from causation, run experiments, count opportunity costs, ignore sunk costs. Unlike Kahneman, Nisbett is optimistic: people trained in these tools use them in daily life, and the training transfers.

## Frameworks Introduced
**The Fundamental Attribution Error and the Power of Situations** — people over-attribute others' behaviour to disposition and under-attribute it to situation. **How**: when a client's staff "resist" the new workflow, look at the situation first: is the tool slower for the first week, does it remove something they were measured on, did nobody tell them why? **Why**: situational fixes are cheap; disposition fixes do not exist. **Failure mode**: diagnosing the prospect ("they're just not ready") instead of the situation ("nobody owns the decision").

**Telling More Than We Can Know (Nisbett & Wilson)** — people do not have introspective access to the causes of their own behaviour; they generate plausible reasons after the fact. **How**: treat a prospect's stated reasons for buying or not buying as hypotheses. "We went with the other agency because of price" may be true or may be confabulated. Look at behaviour (did they ask for a discount, or did they stop replying after the ops lead joined?) over reasons. Likewise, do not trust your own explanation of why a deal closed; run the numbers across deals.

**Statistical Reasoning Tools** — (1) **Law of large numbers**: small samples mislead; one failed automation says almost nothing. (2) **Regression to the mean**: extreme performance is followed by less extreme performance regardless of intervention. (3) **Correlation ≠ causation**, with the specific traps of self-selection and the third variable. (4) **Sample bias**: the clients who talk to you are not the clients who exist. **How**: for every causal claim an owner makes, ask three questions: how many cases, what else changed, who ended up in which group and why.

**Experiments Over Observation; A/B Testing as Default** — the only reliable way to know if a change caused an effect is to randomise. **How**: build A/B tests into client automations where the stakes allow (two outreach templates, two routing rules). Sell this as a feature: "we won't tell you which message works; we'll show you." **Why**: it also protects you from being blamed for regression effects.

**Cost-Benefit Reasoning: Opportunity Cost and the Sunk-Cost Rule** — every decision's true cost is the best alternative forgone; costs already paid are irrelevant to future decisions. **How**: price your service against the opportunity cost of the owner's time, not against software subscriptions. Teach the sunk-cost rule to prospects stuck on a failed tool: "What you spent is gone whether you continue or not; the only question is what the next pound buys."

**Loss Aversion, Endowment Effect, Status Quo Bias, Choice Architecture** — Nisbett's summary of behavioural economics with a practical bent: defaults are powerful, so design them deliberately. **How**: make the recommended plan the default in your proposal; make the pilot the default first step; make opt-in friction low and opt-out friction honest.

**Dialectical Reasoning and Logic** — Western formal logic (contradictions must be resolved) versus East Asian dialectical reasoning (contradictions can be held, the middle way, change is constant). Nisbett argues each catches errors the other misses. **How**: when a prospect holds "AI is the future" and "AI isn't reliable" simultaneously, do not force resolution; both are true in a context, and the proposal should specify the contexts.

## Key Principles
- Ask "compared to what?" for every claim of improvement or failure.
- Reasons people give for behaviour are produced, not retrieved. Weight behaviour over reasons, yours included.
- A single vivid case (the prospect's failed project, your best client) is an anecdote; demand the count.
- Never explain regression to the mean with a cause. A process that was unusually bad last quarter will improve on its own; do not let the client credit or blame the automation for it without a baseline.
- Opportunity cost is the real price. The owner's hour on manual work costs what that hour would have earned selling.
- Sunk costs are not costs. Say so plainly when the prospect is paralysed by a past spend.
- Situations beat dispositions. Change the situation (defaults, ownership, timing) before you try to change people.
- Multiple regression on observational data cannot settle causation; neither can a client's dashboard. Only an experiment does.

## Anti-patterns
- Explaining a client's behaviour by their personality ("the owner is a control freak").
- Accepting self-reported reasons as causes in win/loss reviews.
- Building business cases on correlations from the client's own data without asking what else changed.
- Treating a pilot that happened to coincide with a seasonal uptick as proof.

## Worked Example (applied to Fakhar's business)
Pricing and win/loss analysis for your own agency.
1. Telling more than we can know: pull your last 20 proposals. Ignore the stated reasons for loss ("budget," "timing"). Code the behaviours: did the ops lead join the second call, was a pilot offered, how many days between proposal and reply, did they ask about ownership of the workflows.
2. Law of large numbers: 20 is small; treat any pattern as a hypothesis, not a finding.
3. Correlation vs causation: "deals with a pilot close more." Self-selection: maybe you only offer pilots to warm prospects. Check who got offered a pilot and why.
4. Experiment: for the next 20 proposals, randomise pilot offer (yes/no) within qualified leads. Now you know.
5. Opportunity-cost pricing: in the proposal, compute the owner's hours on the manual process × the owner's effective hourly rate as revenue-generator, not as admin. The number is usually 3 to 5 times what they first imagine.
6. Sunk-cost rule in the objection: "The £15k on the last agency is gone either way. The question is whether the next £6k buys a running workflow or another year of 30 hours a week."
7. Defaults: the proposal shows one recommended plan pre-selected; alternatives are available on request.

## Caveats
- Nisbett's optimism about training transfer is based on his own studies of statistics and economics students; the effect is real but modest and decays without practice.
- The East-West reasoning differences are averages with large within-group variance; do not use them to stereotype a prospect.
- His behavioural-economics chapter inherits the nudge literature's issues: effect sizes in field settings are smaller than early lab results suggested.
- Book 01 (Kahneman) is more pessimistic about self-debiasing; book 10 (Galef) sits between them.
