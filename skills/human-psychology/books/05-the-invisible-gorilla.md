# 05 · The Invisible Gorilla
**Author(s)**: Christopher Chabris, Daniel Simons | **Year**: 2010 | **Rank in category**: 05/10
**Why it's on the list**: limits of attention, memory, confidence in perception

## Core Idea
Everyday intuitions about our own minds are systematically wrong. We believe we see what is in front of us, remember what happened, know how things work, can judge confidence as competence, and can read cause from sequence. Each belief is an illusion with a measurable failure rate. The gorilla experiment (half of viewers counting basketball passes miss a person in a gorilla suit walking through the scene) is the emblem: attention is a spotlight, not a floodlight, and we are blind to our own blindness.

## Frameworks Introduced
**The Six Everyday Illusions** — the book's organising structure:
1. **Illusion of Attention** — we believe we notice everything salient; we notice only what we attend to (inattentional blindness). **How**: assume the owner has not seen the process failures that are not on their dashboard. Walk the process physically or pull logs; ask the people doing the work, not the person describing it.
2. **Illusion of Memory** — we believe memory is a recording; it is reconstructed and confidently wrong (change blindness, distorted flashbulb memories, source confusion). **How**: take written notes on calls and send them back for confirmation within hours; never rely on either party's recollection of what was agreed.
3. **Illusion of Confidence** — we treat confidence as a signal of competence, in ourselves and others; in groups the most confident person, not the most accurate, sets the decision. **How**: when a prospect's ops manager confidently says "that integration is impossible," ask for the specific blocker; when you are confident, ask yourself what evidence would change your mind. In your own pitch, calibrated confidence ("this works in 8 of 10 cases, here is what happens in the other 2") is more trustworthy to sophisticated buyers and more defensible later.
4. **Illusion of Knowledge** — we believe we understand how familiar things work (the illusion of explanatory depth: people cannot draw a working bicycle or explain a toilet). **How**: ask the owner to explain their own process step by step. The gaps that appear are the scope. **Why**: it also gently reduces their overconfidence without you contradicting them.
5. **Illusion of Cause** — we infer causation from correlation, from sequence (post hoc), and from narrative. **How**: "sales dropped after we installed X" is sequence, not cause. Ask what else changed.
6. **Illusion of Potential** — we believe large untapped capacity can be unlocked by simple tricks (brain training, subliminal messages, the Mozart effect). **How**: distrust your own and the market's claims that "AI will unlock 10x." Promise measured, bounded gains.

**Confidence Calibration in Groups** — in group problem-solving, decisions follow the most confident member, and groups do not correct for this. **How**: in a prospect's buying committee, identify who is loud and who is right; direct evidence at the loud one and ask the quiet one for their estimate privately.

## Key Principles
- Expertise does not cure inattentional blindness; experts miss things outside their task focus as readily as novices (the radiologist studies the authors cite). An owner who has run the process for ten years still has not seen what they were not looking for.
- Expectation drives what is noticed; set expectations before a demo so attention lands where you want it.
- Confident people are believed more and are not more often right; price your own confidence honestly and distrust the prospect's.
- The vividness of a memory is not evidence of its accuracy; both you and the client will "remember" the scope differently in month three. Write it down.
- Test the illusion of knowledge in yourself before scoping: can you explain the client's process end to end? If not, you are not ready to quote.
- Correlation stories from the owner are hypotheses, not findings; treat them as candidates for an A/B test.
- Avoid the illusion of potential in your marketing. Buyers burned by "10x" claims punish the next vendor who makes them.

## Anti-patterns
- Selling on confident certainty; it wins the unsophisticated and loses the sophisticated, and it sets up a dissonance crisis at the first glitch.
- Accepting the owner's account of a process as the process.
- Multitasking during discovery calls; your own attention is the instrument, and divided attention misses the gorilla in their answers.
- Building a dashboard that tracks what the owner asked for and nothing else; the failures are in what they did not ask for.

## Worked Example (applied to Fakhar's business)
Discovery for a property-management company that "already has a smooth tenant-onboarding process."
1. Illusion of knowledge test: "Walk me through what happens from signed lease to first rent payment, step by step, including who touches it." The owner stalls at step four; the office manager has three steps the owner did not know exist.
2. Illusion of attention: ask the office manager, "What goes wrong that never reaches the owner?" Two recurring manual reconciliations appear. These are your scope.
3. Illusion of memory: within two hours, send a one-page process map and ask both to correct it. Their corrections differ. Reconcile in writing before quoting.
4. Illusion of confidence (your side): quote with calibrated language. "This design handles the standard case and the two exceptions you described. If a third exception type appears, it routes to Sarah with a flag; we revisit after 30 days."
5. Illusion of cause: the owner says "turnover improved after we switched portals." Ask what else changed that quarter (a new manager was hired). Record both; do not build the business case on the portal story.
6. Illusion of potential: the proposal promises 6 hours a week saved on reconciliation, measured, not "transform your operations."

## Caveats
- The gorilla paradigm itself replicates robustly; inattentional blindness and change blindness are well established.
- The book's confidence findings draw partly on the Dunning-Kruger pattern, whose interpretation is debated (some of the effect is statistical regression and measurement artefact), though the practical point that confidence and accuracy are weakly correlated stands.
- The "illusion of potential" chapter's targets (Mozart effect, brain training) have aged well; the critiques have been vindicated.
- Book 01 (Kahneman) covers the same confidence phenomenon as "illusion of validity"; book 10 (Galef) gives the training to calibrate it.
