# 01 · Thinking, Fast and Slow
**Author(s)**: Daniel Kahneman | **Year**: 2011 | **Rank in category**: 01/10
**Why it's on the list**: essential intro to judgment, System 1/2, uncertainty, cognitive biases

## Core Idea
Judgment runs on two systems. System 1 is fast, automatic, associative, and always on; it produces impressions, feelings, and quick answers from whatever is in front of it. System 2 is slow, effortful, and lazy; it mostly endorses System 1's suggestions rather than checking them. Most errors in business judgment are System 1 answering an easier question than the one asked, with System 2 nodding along.

## Frameworks Introduced
**System 1 / System 2** — two modes of cognition. **How**: when a prospect answers instantly and confidently ("automation doesn't work for us"), treat it as a System 1 impression, not a conclusion. Engage System 2 by asking for specifics: which process, which tool, what broke, what was measured. **Why it works**: System 2 engages when the question is concrete and effort is justified; **failure mode**: pushing System 2 too hard causes fatigue and a "no" that is really exhaustion.

**WYSIATI (What You See Is All There Is)** — System 1 builds the most coherent story from available information and ignores what is missing. **How**: before a call, list what the prospect cannot see (your other clients' outcomes, the cost of their current manual process, the time they are losing). Make the invisible visible with numbers. **Why**: coherence, not completeness, drives confidence. **Failure mode**: you also suffer WYSIATI about the prospect; ask what you are not seeing.

**Prospect Theory** — people evaluate outcomes as gains and losses from a reference point, losses loom roughly twice as large as equivalent gains, and sensitivity diminishes as amounts grow. **How**: (1) identify the prospect's reference point (status quo cost, last vendor's price); (2) frame your offer as avoiding a loss they already feel, not acquiring a gain; (3) keep the number of separate "loss" line items low because each is felt disproportionately; (4) the fourfold pattern: people are risk-averse for likely gains and risk-seeking for likely losses, so a business owner bleeding money on manual work will gamble on a fix, while a comfortable owner will refuse even a good bet. **Failure mode**: framing everything as fear produces flight, not purchase.

**Anchoring and Adjustment** — any number mentioned first pulls the final estimate toward it, even when arbitrary. **How**: state the cost of the problem (hours × hourly rate × months) before you state your price; never let "what do agencies usually charge" be answered first. **Why**: adjustment away from an anchor is effortful and stops too early.

**Substitution and the Affect Heuristic** — when a hard question is asked ("Will this project pay off?"), System 1 substitutes an easy one ("Do I like this person?" or "How do I feel about AI right now?"). **How**: assume the prospect is answering the substitute question; make the easy question work for you (clarity, calm, competence) and then force the hard question into the open with a written ROI model.

**Planning Fallacy and the Outside View** — insiders forecast from their plan; outsiders forecast from the base rate of similar projects. **How**: for every automation scope, state your historical delivery time and overrun distribution, not your plan. Use Klein's **premortem**: "It is six months from now and this failed; write the story." **Why**: it legitimizes doubt that WYSIATI suppresses.

**Experiencing vs Remembering Self; Peak-End Rule** — people remember an experience by its peak and its ending, ignoring duration. **How**: design the engagement so the end is strong (a demo of the finished workflow with the client's own data, a measured before/after), because the remembered ending is what gets referred.

## Key Principles
- Ask about the base rate before the story. Stories are coherent; base rates are true.
- Reduce cognitive strain in everything the prospect reads: simple fonts, short sentences, repeated key phrases. Cognitive ease is read as truth and familiarity.
- Regression to the mean explains most "it worked, then it stopped working" stories; do not accept causal explanations for noise.
- Loss aversion is a tax on every change; price it into your offer with guarantees, reversibility, and a pilot.
- The focusing illusion: whatever the prospect is thinking about right now feels more important than it is. Steer attention deliberately.
- Expert intuition is trustworthy only in regular environments with fast feedback. A business owner's intuition about their own process rarely qualifies; neither does yours about a new vertical.
- Sunk cost: a prospect who spent heavily on a failed tool is reluctant to admit it; give them a frame in which the past spend was learning, not waste.

## Anti-patterns
- Trusting a confident forecast. Confidence measures coherence of the story, not its accuracy.
- Letting the prospect anchor on a competitor's price or a "free tool" before the problem cost is quantified.
- Explaining regression effects with causal stories ("the new hire fixed it").
- Describing outcomes as probabilities when frequencies ("3 of the last 10 clients") are processed more accurately.

## Worked Example (applied to Fakhar's business)
Discovery call with a logistics firm owner. He opens: "We tried an automation agency in 2024, it broke after two months." Treat as System 1 impression plus sunk cost plus loss aversion. Script:
1. Reference point: "What did that cost you, in fees and in the hours your team spent on it?" (Anchor on their existing loss.)
2. Concreteness: "Which step broke: the trigger, the data mapping, or the handoff to a person?" (Force System 2; also surfaces WYSIATI, since most "it broke" stories are one unmaintained node.)
3. Base rate: "Of the last ten workflows we shipped, eight are still running untouched; two needed a rebuild when the client changed CRMs. Here is what we do about that." (Outside view, frequency format.)
4. Reframe loss: "Right now you're paying roughly 30 hours a week at £25 for this. That's about £39k a year you've already decided to lose." Then price.
5. Reversibility: 30-day pilot on one workflow, fixed price, cancel without penalty. (Reduces the perceived loss of the decision.)
6. Premortem close: "If this fails in six months, what will the reason have been?" Record the answer; it is your risk register.

## Caveats
- The social-priming chapter (money priming, the "Florida effect," the Lady Macbeth effect) relies on studies that have largely failed to replicate; Kahneman himself publicly acknowledged in 2017 that he placed too much faith in underpowered priming studies. Do not cite priming effects to clients or use them in copy.
- Ego-depletion findings also failed a large multi-lab replication; treat "decision fatigue" as a loose metaphor.
- Prospect theory, anchoring, loss aversion, and the planning fallacy are robust and replicated, though loss-aversion magnitude varies with stakes and context.
- Book 10 (Galef) and book 08 (Nisbett) offer the self-debiasing training Kahneman was pessimistic about; he held that organizations, not individuals, can be designed to debias.
