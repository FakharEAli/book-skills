# 04 · Mistakes Were Made (but Not by Me)
**Author(s)**: Carol Tavris, Elliot Aronson | **Year**: 2007 (rev. 2015, 2020) | **Rank in category**: 04/10
**Why it's on the list**: self-justification and cognitive dissonance

## Core Idea
Cognitive dissonance is the engine of self-justification. When evidence conflicts with the belief "I am competent and good," people change the evidence, not the belief. Self-justification is not lying; it is the automatic, mostly unconscious editing of memory, blame, and interpretation so that past choices look right. Once a person has taken a step and justified it, the next step in the same direction is easier, and reversing becomes nearly impossible without a face-saving exit.

## Frameworks Introduced
**The Pyramid of Choice** — two people start at the top with nearly identical views and face a small decision; each justifies their choice, which moves them a step down opposite sides; each further justification widens the gap until they stand at opposite corners, certain they were always there. **How**: locate where your prospect is on the pyramid about automation. Someone one step down ("we tried it once") can be walked back with a face-saving reframe; someone at the bottom ("automation is a scam") needs a different entry point entirely (do not argue the belief; create a new, small, low-dissonance experience). **Why it works**: the pyramid predicts that early, small interventions are cheap and late ones are expensive. **Failure mode**: trying to argue someone up the pyramid; argument adds dissonance, which strengthens the justification.

**Naive Realism (Ross & Ward, as used by Tavris & Aronson)** — the conviction that I see the world as it is, so anyone who disagrees is uninformed, irrational, or biased. **How**: expect the prospect to assume your proposal is biased because you sell it. Pre-empt: supply the information they would need to reach your conclusion themselves, and say explicitly what you gain. **Why**: naive realism is disarmed by transparency about interests, not by more claims.

**Memory as Self-Justifying Historian** — memories are revised to fit the current self-image; people remember their past choices as wiser and their past positions as closer to their current ones. **How**: treat "we evaluated this carefully in 2024" as a reconstruction. Ask for artefacts (the old proposal, the Slack thread, the invoice) rather than recollections. **Why**: the artefact forces the dissonance into the open without accusing anyone.

**Blind Spots and the Conflict-of-Interest Illusion** — people believe that their own judgment is immune to incentives while others' judgment is corrupted by them. **How**: never argue that you are unbiased; argue that your incentives are aligned (performance-based fee, pilot, exit clause) and let the structure speak.

**The "Mistakes Were Made" Pattern** — passive-voice, agentless admission that preserves self-image. **How**: when a prospect says "the last project didn't work out," hear the passive voice as a sign of unresolved dissonance about their own role. Do not force ownership; offer an attribution that is accurate and face-saving: "Most of these fail because nobody on the client side owned it after launch. Who would own this one?"

## Key Principles
- The more a person has invested (money, time, public statements), the more they will justify the investment. A prospect who announced "we're going AI-first" to their team is committed; a prospect who quietly tried a tool is not.
- Dissonance is strongest when the decision was freely chosen, irreversible, and public. Reduce the dissonance cost of switching to you by making the first step small, reversible, and private.
- People judge themselves by intentions and others by actions. Prospects explain their own failed implementations by good intentions and vendors' failures by bad character. Give them a situational story about the vendor failure and they will accept it, because it also protects them.
- Admitting a mistake is easier when the culture treats error as information (the authors cite aviation's reporting culture). Model this: open with a mistake you made and what you changed.
- Confirmation bias is dissonance avoidance; once a prospect has said "AI isn't reliable" out loud, they will hunt for confirming evidence. Get them to say the opposite out loud, in their own words, early.
- Escalation of commitment: each step down the pyramid makes the next cheaper. Design the client journey as a sequence of small, justified steps toward you.
- Victims and perpetrators remember the same event differently; in a failed vendor relationship both sides are sure the other was at fault. Do not take the prospect's account of the old vendor at face value when scoping.

## Anti-patterns
- Confronting the prospect with evidence that their past decision was wrong. It will be re-interpreted, not accepted.
- Demanding they admit the failed project was their fault before you help. Nobody takes the exit that requires self-condemnation.
- Treating your own past client failures as entirely the client's fault; you are subject to the same mechanism.
- Offering a "we're different from the last vendor" pitch that implicitly criticises their selection judgment.

## Worked Example (applied to Fakhar's business)
Objection handling: "We spent £15k with an automation agency last year and got nothing."
1. Recognise the position on the pyramid: one or two steps down, publicly committed (they told the team), money sunk. Dissonance is high; naive realism is active ("all agencies oversell").
2. Do not argue. Validate the situational cause: "That's the most common outcome I see, and it's almost never because the owner chose badly. It's usually because the agency built it and left, and nobody in the business had the access or the time to keep it alive."
3. Ask for artefacts, not memories: "Do you still have the scope doc? I'd like to see which workflows were in it."
4. Reframe the sunk cost as paid-for learning: "You now know exactly which process matters and what breaks it. That's the expensive part and it's done."
5. Offer a small, reversible, private step: "Let's rebuild just the one workflow, fixed fee, 30 days, and I'll hand over the admin access and documentation on day one so it can never be hostage again."
6. Align incentives explicitly (blind-spot disarm): "Half the fee is due only if it's still running in 60 days. That's how I'd want it if I were you."
7. Let them say the new belief: "What would it take for you to trust automation again?" Their own words are the start of a new pyramid.

## Caveats
- Dissonance theory is well supported, but the book's narrative examples (recovered-memory therapy, wrongful convictions, the Iraq war) are illustrations, not tests of the theory.
- The authors' claims about police interrogation techniques are well-documented but outside this skill's domain.
- Book 10 (Galef) gives the practices for resisting self-justification in yourself; book 01 (Kahneman) treats sunk cost and loss aversion as the economic face of the same phenomenon.
- Book 06 (Barrett) would describe the "discomfort" of dissonance as constructed affect, not a basic drive; the practical implications are the same.
