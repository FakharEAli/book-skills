# 06 · How Emotions Are Made
**Author(s)**: Lisa Feldman Barrett | **Year**: 2017 | **Rank in category**: 06/10
**Why it's on the list**: theory of constructed emotion (influential, contested)

## Core Idea
Emotions are not hard-wired reactions triggered by events and read off the face; they are constructed in the moment by a predicting brain that combines raw bodily sensation (affect: pleasant or unpleasant, calm or aroused), learned emotion concepts, and context. The same bodily state becomes "anxiety," "excitement," or "indigestion" depending on the concept applied. Because concepts are learned and social, emotions can be re-categorised, and the vocabulary a person has for feelings changes what they feel and do.

## Frameworks Introduced
**The Theory of Constructed Emotion (vs the Classical View)** — the classical view holds that each emotion has a distinct fingerprint in face, body, and brain; Barrett argues evidence for fingerprints is weak and that emotions are instances of concepts constructed from affect plus context. **How**: never infer a prospect's emotion from face or tone alone; a frown on a call is affect (unpleasant, aroused) that could be scepticism, distraction, a bad night, or a prior meeting. Ask, do not read. **Why**: the classical view leads salespeople to "read the room" with confidence and be wrong; the constructionist view says the prospect's own label is the data. **Failure mode**: treating the theory as "emotions aren't real"; they are real, they are just built rather than triggered.

**Affect vs Emotion; Affective Realism** — affect is the constant background feeling of valence and arousal; emotion is affect categorised. **Affective realism**: people experience their affect as a property of the world ("this proposal feels risky" rather than "I feel uneasy and am looking at a proposal"). **How**: separate the two for the prospect. A tired, overloaded owner will perceive any new project as risky; schedule high-stakes calls when their body budget is likely to be in surplus (not Friday 5 pm, not straight after a board meeting) and lower arousal before asking for a decision.

**The Body Budget (allostasis)** — the brain's core job is predictive regulation of bodily resources; chronic deficits (poor sleep, stress, overload) produce unpleasant affect that gets attributed to whatever is in view. **How**: an owner in chronic body-budget deficit is not a bad lead, but their "no" is partly physiological; reduce what you ask them to process (one-page proposals, one decision per call) and remove friction.

**Emotional Granularity** — the ability to construct finely differentiated emotional experiences ("irritated," "apprehensive," "disappointed") rather than coarse ones ("bad"). Higher granularity predicts better regulation and decision-making. **How**: give the prospect precise words for what they feel about the project: "It sounds less like doubt about the technology and more like worry about your team's bandwidth to support it." A precise label is actionable; "bad feeling" is not.

**Recategorisation** — because emotions are constructed, the same affect can be relabelled: pre-exam anxiety as determination, pre-pitch nerves as readiness. **How**: when a prospect names nervousness about change, offer the recategorisation with a reason: "That's the same feeling every owner has at this stage; it's what careful people feel before they commit to something that matters."

## Key Principles
- Ask for the label; do not assign it. "How are you feeling about this so far?" produces better data than any read of body language.
- Context is a component of the emotion, not a modifier. Change the context (a demo on their own data, a call with their ops lead present) and the emotion about your proposal changes.
- Vocabulary is leverage. Teach the prospect's team specific words for the states the new workflow will remove ("reconciliation dread" is a concept they can rally against).
- Body budget before persuasion: do not pitch to someone depleted. Reschedule and you will get a different person.
- Prediction runs the show. People experience what they expect; set expectations about how the project will feel (bumpy fortnight, then relief) so that the bumps are predicted, not alarming.
- The social reality of emotion concepts means your market shares a vocabulary ("AI anxiety," "automation fatigue"). Use it when you want to be understood; replace it when you want to change behaviour.
- Your own affect is also data about your body, not about the deal. A gut feeling that a prospect is "cold" is often your own fatigue.

## Anti-patterns
- Claiming to read a prospect's emotions from micro-expressions or voice; the evidence does not support it and it leads to confident errors.
- Using universal "emotional triggers" in copy as if emotions were buttons; the same stimulus constructs different emotions in different readers.
- Ignoring the prospect's physical state when timing a decision.
- Treating a prospect's expressed emotion as fixed rather than as a construction that can be relabelled.

## Worked Example (applied to Fakhar's business)
Second call with a dental-practice owner who went quiet after the pricing was shown.
1. Do not read it. Ask: "You went quiet when the number came up. What's the feeling, if you had to name it?" She says, "Not sticker shock. More like I'm already behind on three other things and this is one more."
2. Granularity: the feeling is overload, not price resistance. Different remedy.
3. Body budget: she has three open projects and a staff shortage. Reduce load: "What if we handled the setup entirely, you give us 45 minutes total across the month, and nothing goes live without your sign-off?"
4. Recategorise: "The 'one more thing' feeling is accurate right now; in six weeks this is the thing that removes two of the other three."
5. Context change: propose the next call with her practice manager, who owns the processes, so the emotion constructed about the project is built in the right context.
6. Predict the experience: "Week one will feel like more work, because we'll ask questions. Week three it goes quiet because it's running." Set the prediction so the bump is expected.

## Caveats
- This is the most contested book on the list. Basic-emotion theorists (Ekman, Panksepp, Adolphs, Cowen & Keltner) dispute Barrett's reading of the neuroimaging and cross-cultural evidence; there is reasonable support for some universality in expression recognition, and the field has not settled.
- Barrett's practical claims (ask rather than read, granularity helps regulation, body state shapes judgment) are better supported than her strongest theoretical claims (no emotion fingerprints at all).
- Some evidence she uses for affective realism (e.g. judicial decisions varying with time since a meal) has been challenged on methodological grounds.
- Book 03 (Myers) presents the two-factor theory of emotion, which anticipates constructionism; book 09 (Pinker) treats emotions as evolved adaptations with specific functions, which is closer to the classical view.
