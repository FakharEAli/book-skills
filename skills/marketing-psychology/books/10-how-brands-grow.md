# 10 · How Brands Grow: What Marketers Don't Know
**Author(s)**: Byron Sharp | **Year**: 2010 | **Rank in category**: 10/10
**Why it's on the list**: mental & physical availability, double jeopardy, distinctive assets, light buyers; challenges loyalty/differentiation orthodoxy

## Core Idea
Brands grow by acquiring more buyers, mostly **light buyers**, not by deepening loyalty among existing ones. Buyer behaviour follows statistical regularities (the Ehrenberg-Bass "laws") that hold across categories and countries, and most marketing orthodoxy (targeting, differentiation, loyalty programmes) contradicts them. What actually drives growth is **mental availability** (being thought of in buying situations) and **physical availability** (being easy to buy), built through consistent, distinctive, broad-reach marketing.

## Frameworks Introduced
**Mental availability and physical availability** — the two growth levers. Mental availability is the probability of being noticed or thought of in a buying situation, built by linking the brand to the cues present at those moments (later formalised as **category entry points**). Physical availability is being present and easy to buy wherever and whenever the buyer looks. **How**: list the situations in which an owner thinks "we need help with this"; make sure your brand is attached to each cue, and that the path to buying is frictionless at that moment.

**Distinctive brand assets** — non-verbal and verbal elements (colours, logo, tagline, tone, a face) that make the brand recognisable without the name. Sharp separates **distinctiveness** (being recognised) from **differentiation** (being different), and argues buyers barely perceive differentiation; brands in a category are near-substitutes bought from a repertoire. **How**: pick a small set of assets and never change them; measure fame and uniqueness, not "liking". **Failure**: refreshing the look every year.

**The laws of buyer behaviour** (empirical generalisations):
- **Double jeopardy**: smaller brands have fewer buyers *and* those buyers are slightly less loyal. Loyalty is mostly a function of size, not of marketing.
- **Pareto 60/20** (not 80/20): the heaviest 20% of buyers deliver roughly half of sales; light buyers are the majority and must be reached.
- **Natural monopoly**: bigger brands attract a disproportionate share of light, infrequent buyers.
- **Duplication of purchase**: brands share customers with other brands in proportion to those brands' size; there are no "segments" of exclusive buyers.
- **Buyer moderation** (regression to the mean): this period's heavy buyers are lighter next period and vice versa.

**Seven rules of sophisticated mass marketing**: (1) continuously reach all buyers of the category; (2) ensure the brand is easy to buy; (3) get noticed; (4) refresh and build memory structures that make the brand easy to notice and buy; (5) create and use distinctive brand assets; (6) be consistent yet fresh; (7) stay competitive, keep mass appeal, don't give buyers a reason *not* to buy.

## Key Principles
- Publish continuously to the whole category of owners you could serve, not only to "ideal customers", because the next buyer is usually a light, occasional one you didn't target.
- Attach the brand to the *situations* where help is sought (new hire, system migration, month-end chaos), because mental availability is cue-based.
- Fix one visual identity, one tagline, one tone, one face, and keep them for years, because recognition compounds and refreshes reset it.
- Make buying frictionless at the moment of need (one-click booking, visible pricing, fast reply), because physical availability converts mental availability.
- Expect lower loyalty while small; it is double jeopardy, not a service failure.
- Don't give a reason not to buy: remove the jargon, the odd pricing, the unclear scope that excludes a would-be buyer.
- Measure recognition and reach, not engagement rates on individual posts.

## Anti-patterns
- Narrow persona targeting as the whole strategy; it caps reach and ignores light buyers.
- Rebranding, new tagline, new colour scheme every year.
- Loyalty schemes and "retention marketing" as a growth engine for a small brand.
- Spending the message on *differences* the buyer cannot perceive instead of on being noticed and remembered.

## Where the orthodoxy does and doesn't transfer to a small B2B service firm
Sharp's data come from repertoire FMCG markets with millions of light buyers and low involvement. Fakhar's market has a few hundred reachable buyers, long cycles, high stakes and group decisions. Adjustments:
- **Transfers fully**: mental availability (be the name that comes to mind when the owner's process breaks); distinctive assets on LinkedIn and the site; consistency over novelty; "don't give a reason not to buy"; continuous, not burst, presence.
- **Transfers with changes**: "reach all category buyers" becomes *reach every owner in your serviceable segments repeatedly on one channel*, since mass media is out of scope. Light-buyer logic still applies: most future clients aren't engaging with your posts today.
- **Does not transfer**: "differentiation barely matters". In a high-stakes, considered B2B purchase, *believable mechanism* and fit are inspected (07, 05), so meaningful difference matters more than in FMCG. "Loyalty doesn't drive growth": with 10–30 clients, retention and expansion are a large share of revenue; double jeopardy explains churn but does not excuse neglecting it.

## Worked Example (applied to Fakhar's business)
LinkedIn strategy reset.
- **Mental availability**: list eight buying situations ("new ops hire asks for the process", "invoice chasing eats Friday", "CRM migration", "owner wants to step back"). Each becomes a recurring post theme, so the brand is linked to the cue, not to "AI".
- **Distinctive assets**: one colour, one diagram style (the hand-off map), one sign-off line, Fakhar's face on every post; unchanged for 24 months.
- **Reach**: post to the whole segment, five times a week, without narrowing to "ideal" firms; comment on category conversations.
- **Physical availability**: booking link in every post and profile; pricing page public; replies within an hour.
- **Consistency yet fresh**: same assets and themes, new examples weekly.
- **Measure**: profile views and inbound mentions by situation, not likes.

## Caveats
Sharp's laws are robust in FMCG and reasonably supported in services and some B2B categories, but the evidence thins as the number of buyers shrinks and involvement rises. His dismissal of differentiation is the most contested claim; Barden (03) and Berger (07) both show mechanism and fit matter in considered purchases. "Category entry points" are developed properly in *How Brands Grow Part 2* (Romaniuk & Sharp); here they appear in embryo.
