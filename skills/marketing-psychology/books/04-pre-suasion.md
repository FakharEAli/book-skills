# 04 · Pre-Suasion: A Revolutionary Way to Influence and Persuade
**Author(s)**: Robert Cialdini | **Year**: 2016 | **Rank in category**: 04/10
**Why it's on the list**: privileged moments, attention as importance, channeling attention before the message

## Core Idea
What you do *before* delivering a message determines whether it lands. By directing attention to a chosen concept in the moment before the request, you create a **privileged moment** in which the audience is already disposed to agree. The mechanism: **what is focal is presumed causal and important**, and whatever is focal primes the associations the message will be judged against. Persuasion is therefore mostly about arranging attention, not about arguing harder.

## Frameworks Introduced
**Privileged moments** — brief windows when a person is unusually receptive because of what they were just attending to. **How**: (1) decide the single concept your message depends on (e.g. "reliability", "lost time"); (2) put that concept in front of the audience *first*, through a question, image, story or environment; (3) deliver the message while that concept is still active. **Failure mode**: opening with your message and hoping attention follows.

**Attention = importance** — whatever people focus on seems more important and more causal than it is (the focusing illusion). **How**: make the problem you solve the focal object of the first 30 seconds (a diagnostic question, a specific number, a screenshot of their broken process). **Why it works**: the audience then over-weights that problem in the decision.

**Openers that channel attention** — questions and framings that make a trait or need salient before the ask. Cialdini's example: asking "Do you consider yourself adventurous?" before a request raised compliance. **How**: ask a question whose honest answer is a self-description consistent with your offer ("Would you say you're the kind of owner who'd rather fix a process once than firefight it weekly?").

**Attractors and magnetisers** — what pulls attention (the sexual, the threatening, the *self*, the novel) and what holds it (the **unfinished**, and **mystery stories** that pose a puzzle and delay the resolution). **How**: open LinkedIn posts and landing pages with something incomplete or counterintuitive about the reader's own situation; resolve it only after the key idea is delivered.

**Association and priming** — background cues shift evaluation (soft clouds on a furniture site raised preference for comfort; pennies raised price sensitivity). **How**: audit the imagery, metaphors and vocabulary on your site and decks; strip anything that primes "cost", "complexity" or "risk" before the pitch; prime "control", "calm", "done".

**Commitment via the pre-ask question** — asking people whether they'll help, before asking for help, raises the yes rate. **How**: "If I could show you the three places leads are leaking, would you want to see them?" precedes the demo.

## Key Principles
- Decide what the prospect must be thinking about before you speak, because the message is judged against whatever is active at that moment.
- Open every call with a question that makes the problem focal and quantified, because focal problems are judged more important and causal.
- Prime the identity you need ("someone who fixes systems") with a self-description question, because people act consistently with what they just said about themselves.
- Use counterintuitive, unfinished openers in content, because the unresolved holds attention through the body of the post.
- Audit your visual and verbal environment for primes, because background cues move price sensitivity and risk perception without anyone noticing.
- Put trust-building admissions early, because trustworthiness established before the pitch colours everything after it.
- Keep the gap between the pre-suasive opener and the message short, because privileged moments decay quickly.

## Anti-patterns
- Starting a call with your company story; attention is on you, not on the prospect's problem.
- Decks whose first slide primes "technology" and "complexity" (architecture diagrams) before the business outcome.
- Leaving the mystery unresolved; curiosity without payoff feels manipulative.
- Using pre-suasion to extract a yes the person would regret; the 2021 edition of Influence stresses that trust compounds only when the opener is honest.

## Worked Example (applied to Fakhar's business)
Discovery-call opener for a logistics firm owner.
- **Pre-call prime**: calendar invite titled "The 3 hand-offs costing [Company] time", with a one-line agenda; the booking page shows a calm dashboard image, not code.
- **Minute 0–2 (focal problem)**: "Before I say anything about us: when a new booking comes in, how many people touch it before it's confirmed to the customer?" Follow with "and what's that per week, roughly?"
- **Self-description opener**: "Are you the kind of operator who'd rather fix that once, even if it takes six weeks, than keep patching it?"
- **Unfinished hook**: "There's one place most firms your size lose the most time, and it isn't where they look. I'll show you where in a minute."
- **Trust early**: "Some of what you'll describe we won't touch; custom software isn't us."
- **Pre-ask**: "If I could map those hand-offs this week and show you which one to automate first, would you want that?"
- Only now: the offer.

## Caveats
Several priming studies cited have faced replication problems (the "social priming" literature in particular); treat specific effect sizes as unreliable and the general attention-first logic as robust. Pre-suasion works on the opener; the body of the pitch still has to clear the barriers in 07. Sharp (10) would frame "attention" at the brand level (mental availability) rather than the single conversation.
