# 01 · Influence: The Psychology of Persuasion (New and Expanded)
**Author(s)**: Robert B. Cialdini | **Year**: 1984; expanded edition 2021 | **Rank in category**: 01/10
**Why it's on the list**: the 7 principles: reciprocity, commitment/consistency, social proof, liking, authority, scarcity, unity

## Core Idea
People cope with an overloaded world by using shortcuts: a single trigger feature ("click, run") sets off a whole compliance response. Cialdini groups the reliable triggers into seven principles. Each works because it is *usually* a good guide to behaviour, which is exactly why it fires even when the evidence behind it is thin. The ethical use is to make a genuinely present principle visible, not to counterfeit one.

## Frameworks Introduced
**The 7 Principles of Influence** — the compliance triggers.
- *Reciprocity*: we repay what we receive, including uninvited gifts and concessions. Variant: **rejection-then-retreat** (a large request refused, then a smaller one, which is read as a concession and reciprocated). **How**: give first; make it meaningful, unexpected and personalised; let the reciprocity be felt, not invoiced.
- *Commitment and consistency*: once we take a stand we act consistently with it. Strongest when the commitment is **active, public, effortful and freely chosen** (the "foot-in-the-door" and "lowball" patterns exploit this). **How**: get a small, voluntary, stated commitment before a large ask.
- *Social proof*: we decide what's correct by what others do, most strongly under **uncertainty** and when the others are **similar** to us. **How**: show many similar people doing the behaviour; never publicise how many people are *not* doing it.
- *Liking*: we say yes to people we like; drivers are similarity, compliments, familiarity through cooperative contact, attractiveness and association with good things.
- *Authority*: we defer to credible expertise. Credibility = expertise + trustworthiness; trappings (titles, uniforms) substitute for substance. **How**: establish expertise *before* the pitch, through a third party where possible; admit a weakness early to earn trustworthiness.
- *Scarcity*: opportunities seem more valuable when less available; **newly** scarce beats always scarce, and scarcity under **competition** is strongest. Psychological reactance drives it. **How**: use only real limits (capacity, dates), and state the loss, not the gain.
- *Unity* (new in 2021): shared identity ("we") beats mere liking. Built by **being together** (family, place, kinship language) and **acting together** (synchrony, co-creation, asking for *advice* rather than *opinion*).

**Contrast principle** — judgements are relative to what came just before. Show the premium option first; the standard one then looks reasonable.

## Key Principles
- Give before you ask, because an unreciprocated favour is uncomfortable; a free, specific diagnostic creates a debt a generic "free consultation" does not.
- Get small stated commitments early ("so your goal for Q1 is to stop losing leads over the weekend?"), because people defend positions they voiced.
- Show social proof that is **similar** to the prospect (same industry, same size), because an enterprise logo wall does little for a 12-person firm.
- Admit a limitation before the strength ("we don't do custom software; we do workflow automation, and here's why that's what you need"), because trustworthiness is earned by apparent disinterest.
- Have authority introduced by someone else (referrer, LinkedIn headline, case study) rather than asserting it, because self-praise reads as salesmanship.
- Make scarcity truthful and capacity-based ("we onboard two clients a month"), because fabricated deadlines destroy the authority you just built.
- Ask prospects for **advice** on your offer, not their opinion of it, because advice-giving puts them in a "together" state with you.
- Use the contrast principle in proposals: show the three-tier option, premium first.

## Anti-patterns
- Negative social proof ("most firms haven't automated yet") normalises the behaviour you want to end.
- Fake scarcity or countdowns; once seen through, every other claim is discounted.
- Asking for the big commitment without any small prior one.
- Praising the prospect in a way that is clearly instrumental; compliments work only when plausible.

## Worked Example (applied to Fakhar's business)
Cold-to-close sequence for a 30-person accounting firm:
1. **Reciprocity**: send a 90-second Loom auditing one visible process on their site (intake form → no automated follow-up), with one fix they can do themselves.
2. **Authority via third party**: the Loom is introduced by a mutual connection's intro line, or the LinkedIn headline shows "built 40+ intake automations for accountancy firms".
3. **Commitment**: on the call, get them to state the problem in numbers ("so roughly 15 hours a week of manual data entry across the team?").
4. **Social proof, similar**: "a 25-person practice in Leeds had the same intake issue; here's what their first 60 days looked like."
5. **Trustworthiness**: "Honestly, if your volume is under 50 new clients a month this won't pay back; is it above that?"
6. **Scarcity, real**: "we take two new implementations a month; the next slot opens the 20th."
7. **Unity**: "You know your clients better than we do; what would you tell us to avoid?"

## Caveats
Several classic experiments (e.g. some foot-in-the-door and lowball studies) have small samples and mixed replications; treat effect sizes as directional. The principles are stronger for low-involvement, fast decisions than for a £20k B2B engagement, where they set the tone but do not substitute for a believable mechanism (see 07). Shotton (02) argues negative social proof is the most common real-world misuse.
