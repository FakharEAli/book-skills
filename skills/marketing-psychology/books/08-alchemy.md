# 08 · Alchemy: The Surprising Power of Ideas That Don't Make Sense
**Author(s)**: Rory Sutherland | **Year**: 2019 | **Rank in category**: 08/10
**Why it's on the list**: psycho-logic, costly signalling, the opposite of a good idea can be a good idea

## Core Idea
Humans run on **psycho-logic**, which is not irrational but obeys rules that differ from economic logic. Because every competitor can follow logic, logical solutions carry no advantage; the valuable moves are the ones that look silly on a spreadsheet and work in a human. Perception is the product: change how something is perceived and you have changed what it is. Sutherland's method is to ask the "stupid" question, test the counterintuitive thing, and treat signalling as a feature rather than waste.

## Frameworks Introduced
**Psycho-logic** — the hidden rules behind choices that look irrational: we buy signals of trustworthiness, reduce anxiety rather than time, and value what is costly. **How**: for any buyer hesitation, ask "what is the psycho-logical reason, not the economic one?" (e.g. the owner isn't worried about the fee, they're worried about looking foolish to their team).

**Costly signalling** — a message is credible in proportion to how costly it would be to fake. Expensive advertising, generous guarantees, visible effort and time all signal confidence. **How**: spend visibly on things that would be ruinous if you weren't good (unconditional pilot refund, a free hand-built audit, a named engineer on call). **Why it works**: cheap talk is free, so buyers discount it; cost is the proof.

**Sutherland's rules** (his own list, lightly condensed):
1. The opposite of a good idea can also be a good idea.
2. Don't design for average.
3. It doesn't pay to be logical if everyone else is being logical.
4. The nature of our attention affects the nature of our experience.
5. A flower is simply a weed with an advertising budget.
6. The problem with logic is that it kills off magic.
7. A good guess which stands up to observation is still science; so is a lucky accident.
8. Test counterintuitive things, only because no one else will.
9. Solving problems using rationality is like playing golf with only one club.
10. Dare to be trivial.
11. If there were a logical answer, we would have found it already.
**How**: when a problem has resisted logical fixes, generate the opposite of the obvious answer and test it cheaply.

**Reframing the metric** — the Eurostar example: instead of spending billions to shave minutes off the journey, spend far less making the journey pleasant. The Uber map reduces the *anxiety* of waiting, not the wait. **How**: ask whether the buyer's real complaint is the measured quantity or the feeling around it; often the feeling is cheaper to fix.

**The doorman fallacy** — reducing a role to its narrow function (a doorman opens doors; replace with automatic doors) and losing the unmeasured value (security, status, recognition). **How**: when automating, ask what else the human step was quietly doing; preserve or replace it, or the client will feel the loss without naming it.

**Placebo and the alchemy of words** — things work partly because they are believed to; names, rituals and presentation change efficacy. **How**: name your workflows and your process; a named method is trusted more than an unnamed one.

## Key Principles
- When the logical pitch isn't working, try its opposite, because every competitor already tried the logical one.
- Make your confidence costly to fake (guarantee, refund, visible hand-built work), because cheap claims are discounted to zero.
- Fix the anxiety, not just the metric: progress visibility, a weekly check-in and a kill-switch may matter more than another 10% efficiency.
- Before automating a step, list what the human doing it was also providing (relationship, judgement, a sense of being looked after), because the doorman fallacy is how automations get quietly rolled back.
- Don't design for the average client; design for a specific type and let the others self-select.
- Name things: a "Hand-off Audit" is bought; "a review of your processes" is not.
- Test the trivial and the odd, because they are cheap and nobody else does.

## Anti-patterns
- Competing on logic (features, ROI tables) alone; it is a commodity game.
- Stripping out "wasteful" signals (design, packaging, hospitality) because they don't appear in the ROI.
- Automating away a human step without replacing its hidden functions.
- Letting the spreadsheet veto an idea before a cheap test has run.

## Worked Example (applied to Fakhar's business)
Pricing decision: a prospect says a competitor quoted half your price.
- **Psycho-logic**: the owner's hidden question is "which of these won't embarrass me?", not "which is cheaper?".
- **Costly signalling**: hold the price; add a signal the cheap competitor can't afford: "If the first workflow isn't live and used by your team in 30 days, we refund the setup fee." Include a hand-written one-page map of their process from the discovery call.
- **Opposite of the good idea**: instead of cutting scope to meet the price, add a visible, low-cost, high-perceived-value element (weekly 15-minute check-in for the first quarter; a named engineer).
- **Reframe the metric**: emphasise the anxiety you remove (a dashboard that shows every automation ran today) rather than hours saved alone.
- **Doorman check**: the admin who currently handles bookings also notices when a customer sounds unhappy; the automation flags tone and routes to a human, so that value isn't lost.
- **Naming**: the offer is "the Quiet Back Office programme", not "automation retainer".

## Caveats
Sutherland argues by anecdote and inversion; his claims are provocations to test, not findings. "Costly signalling" is well supported in biology and economics, but which signals work in a given market is empirical. He is rhetorically hostile to measurement, yet his own advice is to test; read him as "measure the right thing", not "don't measure". Sharp (10) would caution that cleverness is no substitute for reach and consistency.
