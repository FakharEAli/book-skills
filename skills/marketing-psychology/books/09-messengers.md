# 09 · Messengers: Who We Listen To, Who We Don't, and Why
**Author(s)**: Stephen Martin, Joseph Marks | **Year**: 2019 | **Rank in category**: 09/10
**Why it's on the list**: hard messengers (socio-economic position, competence, dominance, attractiveness) vs soft (warmth, vulnerability, trustworthiness, charisma)

## Core Idea
Whether a message lands depends heavily on *who* delivers it, often more than on its content. Audiences assess the messenger in two broad ways: **hard** traits that confer status (we listen because they are above us) and **soft** traits that create connection (we listen because they are with us). Each of the eight traits has specific cues, and each is favoured by a different context. The practical task is to know which messenger the situation calls for and to signal those traits deliberately.

## Frameworks Introduced
**Hard messengers (status)** — we defer because of perceived position.
- **Socio-economic position**: wealth and status cues (clients, office, pricing) make people listen; **How**: premium signals, named client tiers, a confident price.
- **Competence**: expertise, confidence and, notably, **potential**: audiences are often more excited by promise than by achievement. **How**: show expertise through demonstration (a live diagnosis) and talk about where the client *will be*; failure mode: the competence trap of over-claiming, which turns into distrust when exposed.
- **Dominance**: forceful, decisive messengers are preferred in **uncertain or threatening** contexts; **How**: decisive recommendations ("do this first, not that") when the owner is anxious; failure mode: dominance in a cooperative context reads as arrogance.
- **Attractiveness**: physical and presentational polish is read as competence; **How**: consistent, well-designed touchpoints.

**Soft messengers (connection)** — we listen because we feel bonded.
- **Warmth**: kindness, smiling, positive regard; **How**: lead with the prospect's situation, give unprompted help.
- **Vulnerability**: showing weakness or asking for help invites connection (the pratfall effect); **How**: admit what you don't do, share a mistake and its fix.
- **Trustworthiness**: judged by consistency, by acting against self-interest, and by admitting limits early; **How**: recommend the smaller option when it fits, tell them when not to buy.
- **Charisma**: vision, energy and the ability to make others feel significant; **How**: a clear picture of the client's future state, told with conviction.

**Context rule** — hard messengers win in competitive, hierarchical or uncertain situations; soft messengers win in cooperative, relational, long-term ones. Most B2B service relationships need **competence + trustworthiness** as the base pair, with dominance only when the buyer is frozen.

## Key Principles
- Decide which two traits each touchpoint must signal, because trying to signal all eight dilutes them.
- Establish competence by *doing* a small piece of the work in front of them (live audit), because demonstrated expertise beats claimed expertise.
- Talk about potential ("where you'll be in 90 days") as well as track record, because audiences over-weight promise.
- Signal trustworthiness by recommending against yourself at least once per sale, because acting against self-interest is the strongest trust cue.
- Show a flaw after showing competence, not before, because the pratfall effect only helps the already-competent.
- Use decisive, dominant language when the owner is anxious and indecisive, and warm, collaborative language when they are relaxed and exploring.
- Keep every visual touchpoint polished and consistent, because presentation is read as competence.
- Borrow hard-messenger status through association: who you've worked with, who refers you, who you're quoted by.

## Anti-patterns
- Soft-messenger humility in a context that needs decisiveness (an owner asking "just tell me what to do").
- Dominance as default; it suppresses the information you need from the prospect.
- Over-claiming competence (the competence trap); a single exposed exaggeration zeroes trust.
- Vulnerability before any competence is established, which reads as incompetence.

## Worked Example (applied to Fakhar's business)
Designing the agency's LinkedIn profile and first-call persona.
- **Headline (competence + socio-economic)**: "We run the back office for 30 service firms. Intake, invoicing, hand-offs, done without anyone remembering."
- **About (potential + charisma)**: a vivid picture of the owner's Monday in 90 days, not a biography.
- **Featured (trustworthiness)**: a post titled "Three cases where we told a prospect not to hire us".
- **Posts (vulnerability after competence)**: "the workflow we got wrong in month one and how we fixed it", posted after a run of demonstrated results.
- **First call (context-aware)**: open warm and curious; if the owner is anxious and asking for direction, switch to decisive ("Start with intake. Not invoicing. Here's why."); if exploratory, stay collaborative and offer a menu (07).
- **Association**: name the referrer in the first line; mention the biggest comparable client once, early.

## Caveats
Much of the underlying research is correlational or lab-based (attractiveness and competence judgements, leadership-preference studies); treat as directional. The eight-trait taxonomy is the authors' synthesis, not a validated scale. Trait effects interact with the audience's own status and culture. Cialdini (01) folds several of these into authority and liking; Martin and Marks give the finer grain.
