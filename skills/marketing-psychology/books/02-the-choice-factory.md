# 02 · The Choice Factory
**Author(s)**: Richard Shotton | **Year**: 2018 | **Rank in category**: 02/10
**Why it's on the list**: 25 behavioural biases applied to marketing with examples

## Core Idea
Marketing works better when it is built on how people actually decide rather than on how they say they decide. Shotton takes 25 biases from behavioural science, shows each with a (usually small, self-run) experiment, and insists that the practitioner's job is to **test** them in their own context rather than trust the literature. The tone is anti-orthodoxy: most marketing "best practice" was never tested.

## Frameworks Introduced
**The 25 biases** (selection most useful here; chapter names are Shotton's):
- **Fundamental attribution error** — we over-credit personality and under-credit context. **How**: target *moments and contexts* (end of quarter, after a hire quits, during a software migration) rather than demographic personas. **Why**: the same owner is a different buyer when stressed than when relaxed.
- **Social proof / negative social proof** — "most people do X" works; "most people fail to do X" normalises failure. **How**: quantify adoption in the prospect's segment; never lead with "most SMEs haven't adopted AI".
- **Habit** — behaviour is mostly repeated; habits break at **life events** (new job, relocation, new system). **How**: reach owners when something has just changed: new hire, new premises, new software, a funding round.
- **Pain of payment** — paying hurts, and the salience of payment matters more than the amount. **How**: decouple payment from consumption (monthly retainer vs invoice-per-task), bundle, avoid itemised line items on the proposal.
- **Price relativity** — value is judged against reference points you supply. **How**: anchor against the cost of the problem (salary hours) and against a deliberately premium tier.
- **Expectancy theory** — expectations shape the experience itself; a dearer wine tastes better. **How**: a confident price and a polished handover make the delivered automation *feel* better.
- **Pratfall effect** — admitting a flaw makes you more attractive, if you are otherwise competent. **How**: "we're slower to start than freelancers because we document everything first."
- **The danger of claimed data** — people cannot accurately report why they buy. **How**: never redesign the offer based on survey answers; watch behaviour (reply rates, booked calls).
- **Media context** — the same message lands differently depending on where it appears. **How**: a LinkedIn post read between work tasks needs a faster payoff than a long-form case study.
- **Primacy effect** — first information anchors the rest. **How**: open the proposal with the strongest number.
- **Cocktail party effect** — we notice our own name and group. **How**: industry-specific headlines beat generic "for businesses".
- **Veblen goods** — for some purchases a higher price *is* the appeal; price signals quality.
- **Replicability crisis / variability** — some famous effects are weaker than reported; results differ by context. **How**: run cheap A/B tests before scaling any claim.
- Others covered: mood, confirmation bias, overconfidence, wishful seeing, curse of knowledge (see 05), Goodhart's law, winner's curse, power of the group, distinctiveness (see 10), scarcity (see 01).

## Key Principles
- Design for context, not for personas, because the owner's state at the moment of contact predicts response better than their traits.
- Hunt for life-event triggers in your prospecting (new CFO, new CRM, new office), because habits are only loosely held then.
- Phrase adoption positively and specifically ("61 of the 90 agencies we audited already automate invoicing"), because negative social proof backfires.
- Reduce payment salience: one monthly figure, outcomes described, no hourly ledger.
- State a real flaw early, because it makes the strengths believable (also 01, 09).
- Price with a reference point in the same sentence, never a bare number.
- Trust behaviour over stated preference; a prospect's "too expensive" is a claim, not a diagnosis.
- Test before you believe, because the effects in this book vary with context and some have shrunk under replication.

## Anti-patterns
- Market-research surveys as offer design input (claimed data).
- "Awareness" campaigns that announce how under-adopted your category is.
- Itemised, hourly proposals that maximise pain of payment.
- Copying a competitor's claim because it "worked for them" without testing.

## Worked Example (applied to Fakhar's business)
LinkedIn outreach redesign for a lead-qualification automation offer:
- **Trigger (habit / FAE)**: filter for companies that posted a sales-ops or SDR hire in the last 30 days; their process is in flux.
- **Cocktail party**: subject/open line names the segment: "for 10–50 person recruitment agencies".
- **Social proof, positive**: "Four of the agencies we worked with last quarter now reply to inbound leads in under five minutes."
- **Pratfall**: "We're not cheap and we won't build you a chatbot. We fix the hand-offs."
- **Price relativity**: "Your SDR costs ~£3,200/month. The first-response automation is £900/month."
- **Pain of payment**: single monthly figure; no setup line item visible until later.
- **Test**: run two openers (positive social proof vs. pratfall) across 50 messages each; keep whichever books more calls.

## Caveats
Shotton's experiments are small, often online panels; they prove plausibility, not effect size. He is explicit that several biases interact and can cancel. B2B decision groups dilute some individual-level effects. Sharp (10) would say distinctiveness and reach matter more than any single nudge.
