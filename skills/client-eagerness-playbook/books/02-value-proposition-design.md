# 02 · Value Proposition Design
**Author(s)**: Alexander Osterwalder, Yves Pigneur, Greg Bernarda, Alan Smith | **Year**: 2014 | **Rank in category**: 02/10
**Why it's on the list**: Positioning says who; this says what they actually need done and what hurts. It forces interviews before you build the package.
**Artifact this stage produces**: a Value Proposition Canvas for ONE client segment, filled from buyer interviews.
**Fuller treatment**: sibling skill `offer-creation` → [`02-value-proposition-design.md`](../../offer-creation/books/02-value-proposition-design.md)

## Core Idea
A value proposition is the bundle of benefits a segment can expect from you. It only works when it is designed against an explicit picture of that segment's jobs, pains and gains, and tested against real customers rather than assumed. The canvas is the picture; the interviews and experiments are the test.

## The Framework You Use Here
**Value Proposition Canvas** (Osterwalder et al.) — two sides, filled left side first:
- **Customer Profile** (left)
  - **Customer jobs** — what they are trying to get done: functional (quote every enquiry same day), social (look organised to clients), emotional (stop dreading Monday admin), plus supporting jobs (buying, co-creating, transferring).
  - **Pains** — obstacles, risks and bad outcomes around those jobs. Rank by **severity**: extreme ↔ moderate.
  - **Gains** — required, expected, desired and unexpected outcomes. Rank by **relevance**: essential ↔ nice to have.
- **Value Map** (right)
  - **Products & services** — what you offer, listed concretely.
  - **Pain relievers** — how each item kills or reduces a ranked pain.
  - **Gain creators** — how each item produces a ranked gain.
- **Fit** — achieved when pain relievers and gain creators address the pains and gains the customer ranks highest. The authors distinguish problem-solution fit (on paper, from interviews), product-market fit (evidence customers care, in the market) and business model fit (it is profitable). Stage 02 reaches problem-solution fit; stages 03–10 test the rest in real conversations.

**How to fill it**: 5–8 interviews with people who match the stage 01 best-fit description. Ask about the last time the job went wrong, what it cost, what they tried. Write pains in their words. Only then draw the right side, and draw it thin: an agency that lists eight services on the right side has not chosen.

## Decision Rules for This Stage
- Interview before you fill in a single pain. A pain you wrote yourself is a hypothesis; mark it as one.
- Keep only the top three pains by severity for the offer. The fourth becomes a bonus or a second project, never scope.
- A pain with no expensive consequence (hours, money, lost customers, risk) is not the problem to build the offer around; your rule is one *expensive* operational problem.
- If interviewees describe the pain differently from each other, the segment is really two segments; narrow it.

## Anti-patterns
- Filling the Value Map first and then hunting for pains that justify it.
- Treating gains as the inverse of pains. "Faster" is a gain only if the interviewee said speed mattered.
- Skipping emotional and social jobs. Owners buy relief from worry and from looking disorganised more often than they buy throughput.

## Worked Example
Segment from stage 01: owner-run fitness studios.
- Jobs: answer every enquiry same day (functional); appear responsive and professional (social); stop feeling guilty about DMs left unread (emotional).
- Pains (ranked by severity): 1 enquiries answered hours later and lost to the studio down the road; 2 desk staff distracted from members in the room; 3 no idea how many enquiries died.
- Gains (ranked by relevance): 1 more trials booked; 2 knowing the number every week; 3 staff not touching the phone during sessions.
- Products & services: enquiry agent; weekly lost/recovered report; onboarding and tuning.
- Pain relievers: answer in under a minute; agent handles the conversation so the desk does not; the report makes the lost number visible.
- Gain creators: trial booked directly in their calendar; Monday summary.
Fit check: pains 1–3 each have a reliever; nothing on the right side exists without a left-side reason.

## Caveats
The book is a design toolkit, not a research method; its interview guidance is light. Pair it with the Problem and Implication questions in [04](04-spin-selling.md) when you interview. Rankings from six interviews are directional, not statistical.
