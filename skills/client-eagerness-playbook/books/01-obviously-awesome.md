# 01 · Obviously Awesome
**Author(s)**: April Dunford | **Year**: 2019 | **Rank in category**: 01/10
**Why it's on the list**: Before you can make anyone eager, you have to say who it is for, what they use today, and why they should choose you. Everything downstream inherits this.
**Artifact this stage produces**: a positioning statement for ONE segment.
**Fuller treatment**: sibling skill `offer-creation` → [`03-obviously-awesome.md`](../../offer-creation/books/03-obviously-awesome.md)

## Core Idea
Positioning is the context you set so a buyer understands what you are, why it matters, and who it is for. Dunford's argument: most positioning fails because founders describe the product the way they built it, not against the alternatives the buyer would actually use instead. Positioning is deliberately chosen, not inherited from the first market you tried.

## The Framework You Use Here
**Positioning components** (Dunford) — work through them in this order, because each one is derived from the one before:
1. **Competitive alternatives** — what the best-fit customer would do if you did not exist. For AI automation that is usually: a part-time admin, a spreadsheet plus WhatsApp, an offshore VA, a generic SaaS tool, or "nothing, we live with it". Not "other AI agencies" unless the buyer is already shopping agencies.
2. **Unique attributes** — what you have that the alternatives lack. Be literal: a workflow that runs unattended overnight; an agent that reads the inbox and books the job; a research pipeline that drafts before the team wakes.
3. **Value** — what those attributes enable for the customer, stated as outcomes (hours back, fewer missed leads, faster quotes), grouped into two or three value themes.
4. **Best-fit customers** — the characteristics of the buyers who care most about that value. Owner-operated, 5–50 staff, high enquiry volume, one person doing the admin, etc.
5. **Market category** — the frame of reference that makes the value obvious. Dunford names three styles: **head to head** (compete in an existing category), **big fish, small pond** (a sub-segment of an existing category) and **create a new game**. For a small agency, big-fish-small-pond is nearly always the right pick: "AI back-office automation for [industry]".
6. **Relevant trends** (optional) — a trend layered on top to explain why now; never used as the category itself.

## Decision Rules for This Stage
- List alternatives by asking buyers "what would you do instead?", not by listing competitors you know. If your alternatives list has no "do nothing / hire a person" entry, it is wrong.
- A unique attribute that no alternative lacks is not unique; drop it or it dilutes the statement.
- If you cannot name the best-fit customer in one sentence, the segment is too broad for stage 02's interviews.
- Pick the category the buyer already has budget language for ("operations", "admin", "lead handling") rather than the category you want to be in ("AI agents").

## Anti-patterns
- Positioning against "AI tools" in general: buyers do not have an AI budget; they have an admin budget.
- Trying to serve two segments with one statement. The process is one offer, one industry; a second segment gets its own run through the sequence later.
- Treating positioning as a tagline. The statement is an internal decision document; the tagline comes at stage 10.

## Worked Example
Segment: independent gyms and fitness studios, 1–3 locations.
- Alternatives: front-desk staff answering DMs between sessions; a generic booking app with no follow-up; missed enquiries left unanswered.
- Unique attributes: an agent that answers Instagram/WhatsApp enquiries within a minute, qualifies, and books a trial; a weekly report of enquiries lost and recovered.
- Value: trials booked from enquiries that previously died; front desk freed for members in the room.
- Best fit: owner-run studios with >40 enquiries a month and no dedicated sales person.
- Category: "enquiry-to-trial automation for independent studios".
Statement: *For owner-run fitness studios drowning in unanswered DMs, we are the enquiry-to-trial automation that books trials while the desk is busy, unlike a booking app that waits to be found.*

## Caveats
Dunford writes for software companies with a product to position; a services agency can reposition faster and should re-run this stage after every ten discovery calls. She is explicit that positioning is a team exercise; as a founder-led agency you substitute buyer interviews (stage 02) for the internal team.
