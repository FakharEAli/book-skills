# 06 · Influence: The Psychology of Persuasion
**Author(s)**: Robert Cialdini | **Year**: 1984 (revised 2021) | **Rank in category**: 06/10
**Why it's on the list**: Once trust behaviours are planned, check what your website and proposal actually assert. Cialdini's principles are the taxonomy for sorting claims into evidenced and unevidenced.
**Artifact this stage produces**: an evidence audit of website and proposal: every claim tagged with the proof behind it, or marked for removal.
**Fuller treatment**: sibling skill `marketing-psychology` → [`01-influence.md`](../../marketing-psychology/books/01-influence.md)

## Core Idea
People use shortcuts to decide whom to say yes to, and those shortcuts respond to specific cues. Cialdini describes them as levers that can be used ethically (when the cue reflects a real underlying fact) or exploited (when it is faked). In this process the principles are used in one direction only: to check whether each claim you make is backed by a real cue, and to remove claims that are not.

## The Framework You Use Here
**Principles of influence** (Cialdini) — reciprocity, commitment and consistency, social proof, authority, liking, scarcity; the 2021 edition adds unity. For the audit, each becomes a proof type:
- **Social proof** — named clients in the same segment, specific results, quotes with names and roles. "Trusted by 50+ businesses" with no names is an assertion, not proof.
- **Authority** — demonstrated expertise: a published process, a teardown, a working demo, a recognised credential. A logo wall of tools you use is not authority.
- **Commitment and consistency** — small, voluntary steps the prospect takes (a scoping session, sharing their enquiry data) that make the larger yes consistent with what they have already done. In the audit: does the proposal offer a small first step?
- **Reciprocity** — something of real value given first: a free audit of their enquiry handling, a sample report run on their data. In the audit: is anything given before anything is asked?
- **Liking** — similarity and genuine compliments; in B2B services this is mostly "we have worked with people exactly like you" backed by the social-proof entries.
- **Scarcity** — only legitimate when true (your capacity is genuinely limited to N builds a month). Audit rule: any scarcity claim you cannot document is removed.
- **Unity** — shared identity ("we run a small business too"). Weak unless specific.

## Decision Rules for This Stage
- Extract every claim from the website and the current proposal into a list. A claim is any sentence a sceptical owner could reply "says who?" to.
- Tag each with one proof type and the specific evidence (client name, number, artefact). No evidence → mark **remove** or **downgrade** to a description of what you do.
- Prefer one specific proof over three vague ones. A single named studio with a number beats "proven results".
- Evidence that also lowers self-orientation (stage 05) ranks highest: a public process, a free sample report, a stated "we are not a fit if…".
- Never add a principle to a page as a technique. Only add evidence you can show.

## Anti-patterns
- Fabricated or borrowed scarcity and urgency; conflicts with [05](05-the-trusted-advisor.md) and [09](09-the-jolt-effect.md).
- Testimonials without name, role and segment; they read as filler to operators.
- Authority by association: lists of AI models, platforms, or "as seen in" badges from unrelated contexts.

## Worked Example
Proposal audit, three lines of many:
| Claim | Proof type | Evidence | Action |
|---|---|---|---|
| "Replies in under a minute, 7 days a week" | Authority (demonstrable) | Live demo on their own Instagram test account | Keep; link the demo |
| "Studios see more trials booked" | Social proof | None yet in this segment | Downgrade to "designed to…" until first studio result exists |
| "Limited to 3 new builds this month" | Scarcity | True this month: capacity plan | Keep only while true; remove the line otherwise |

## Caveats
Several of the classic studies Cialdini cites have been questioned in the replication literature; the principles remain useful as a checklist of what buyers look for, not as guaranteed levers. The book is about persuasion, and the process here deliberately uses it as a defensive audit.
