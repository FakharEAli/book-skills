# 07 · Selling the Invisible
**Author(s)**: Harry Beckwith | **Year**: 1997 | **Rank in category**: 07/10
**Why it's on the list**: An automation that does not exist yet cannot be inspected. Beckwith's whole book is about what buyers judge instead, and how to give them something to judge.
**Artifact this stage produces**: an evaluability plan: the demos, process descriptions and examples a prospect can inspect before buying.
**Fuller treatment**: sibling skill `sales-psychology` → [`07-selling-the-invisible.md`](../../sales-psychology/books/07-selling-the-invisible.md)

## Core Idea
A service is a promise, not a thing. Because prospects cannot test it before purchase, they decide on the visible proxies: how you answer the phone, what your proposal looks like, whether your process is explained, what the first and last impressions were. Beckwith's instruction is to fix the service first, then make every part of it visible, and to accept that the prospect's primary motive is to avoid a bad choice rather than to find the best one.

## The Framework You Use Here
Beckwith writes in short essays rather than named systems; the stage uses four of his recurring instructions:
- **Make the invisible visible** — give the buyer artefacts: a written process with named steps, a sample output on their own data, a recorded demo, a one-page "what happens in week one". Every artefact lowers perceived risk.
- **Focus (the anchor)** — be known for one thing in one segment; a narrow claim is more believable than a broad one. This is Dunford's positioning restated for services, and it is why the process is one offer.
- **Satisfaction is expectations minus delivery** — set expectations explicitly (what the agent will and will not handle, what week one looks like) so delivery can exceed them. Over-promising in the proposal creates dissatisfaction even when the build is good.
- **First and last impressions** — the enquiry response time, the proposal's look, the handover call and the final report are judged out of proportion to their effort. Design them deliberately.

He also argues that buyers of services fear the downside more than they desire the upside; evidence that reduces fear (process, references, guarantee) outweighs evidence of brilliance.

## Decision Rules for This Stage
- For every stage 06 claim that lacked evidence, create an artefact a prospect can inspect: demo, process page, example output, reference call.
- Build one demo on the prospect's own channel or data before the proposal; a generic demo is a feature, a demo on their data is a result they can picture.
- Publish the process. If a prospect can describe your week one back to you, option overload (stage 09) falls.
- Write the "not for you if…" list. A visible boundary is itself evaluable evidence of focus.
- Respond to every enquiry about your own service faster than the response time you sell. You are the first demo.

## Anti-patterns
- Describing capabilities ("we build agents on n8n and LLM APIs") instead of showing outputs. Tools are invisible to an owner.
- Hiding the process to avoid being copied. The process is the thing they can evaluate; copying is not your bottleneck.
- Letting the last impression be an invoice. The final report or handover is where the next project is decided.

## Worked Example
Evaluability plan for "Every Enquiry Answered":
| What they can inspect | Format | When |
|---|---|---|
| The agent answering a real DM to a test account on their Instagram | Live screen-share, 10 minutes | Scoping session |
| Week-one plan: day-by-day, who does what | One page, in the proposal | Proposal |
| Sample weekly lost/recovered report | Filled with their last month's enquiries, anonymised | Proposal |
| Handover: what the agent will not do and who it hands to | Named list | Proposal and go-live |
| Reference: one studio owner willing to take a call | Intro email | On request |
| Your own response time to their enquiry | Measured, mentioned once | First contact |

## Caveats
The book predates the web as a sales channel and offers anecdotes rather than data; its value is the discipline of asking "what can they see?", not its examples. It overlaps heavily with [05](05-the-trusted-advisor.md) and [06](06-influence.md); use it as the implementation step for both.
